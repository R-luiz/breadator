Slice the cooled loaf and look at the crumb: it records how the fermentation went and shows up errors of technique [@breadcode-1018; @breadcode-2922]. Flat, dense or gummy loaves mostly come from a fermentation that ran too short or too long, and three checks settle most cases: the starter, the bulk fermentation, the final proof [@breadcode-2983; @breadcode-2984].

## Too short, about right, too long

| | Too short | About right | Too long |
|---|---|---|---|
| Crumb | Unbaked, gummy areas towards the bottom | Evenly baked, moist but not gummy | Can feel gummy: most of the gluten is broken down |
| Alveoli | Overly large "craters" | Evenly distributed, no craters | Many tiny ones, evenly spread |
| Taste | Pale, neutral | Balanced, vinegary or lactic depending on the starter | Strongly acidic |
| Oven spring | Upwards, but mostly from evaporating water | Strong and upwards | Flat, like a pancake |

The table is adapted from Table 7.2 of *The Sourdough Framework*, written for wheat sourdough [@breadcode-0490; @breadcode-0491; @breadcode-0492].

The words differ between sources. The Framework finds "underproofed" misleading: the cause usually lies in the bulk fermentation or in the starter, and a well-timed bulk gives a good crumb even with no final proof [@breadcode-1045; @breadcode-1047]. Novita Listyani speaks of under- and over-proofing for the same defects, gaping holes with a gummy crumb on one side, collapse in the oven on the other [@novita-0691]. Both advise the same side to err on: stop a little early rather than late [@breadcode-0607; @novita-0691].

## Dense, with a few large holes

This is the under-fermented loaf, sometimes called fool's crumb: a loaf with an ear but several overly large pockets in the crumb [@breadcode-1050; @breadcode-2920]. The gummy patches are places where the dough never inflated. Alveoli help heat to pass, so these areas stay raw-looking, and baking longer to fix them overbakes the rest [@breadcode-2267; @breadcode-1051].

- Lengthen the bulk fermentation rather than shaping sooner [@breadcode-1317].
- Check the starter: a weak or immature one gives a dense, flat or gummy loaf [@breadcode-2947]; [Maintaining a starter](/en/learn/starter-maintenance) shows how to tell when it is ready.
- With a scald (tangzhong) dough at very high hydration, the poke test can mislead, and under-proofing then shows as tunnels and a gummy crumb [@novita-0756].
- In a 100 % whole-wheat loaf, Novita's first suspect is a low-protein flour; she uses about 13.5 % protein or more [@novita-0032].

## Flat, sticky and sour

This is the over-fermented loaf. Acid and enzymes have broken down the gluten [@breadcode-1575; @breadcode-1027], the gas disperses, and what is left is a flat loaf with small alveoli and a sour taste [@breadcode-1028; @breadcode-0970]. The dough warns you before the bake: it tears when folded or shaped, turns very sticky, clings to the banneton and flattens when turned out [@breadcode-2567; @breadcode-1032]. The stickiness has a cause: protein breakdown and acid release water that was bound [@novita-0101]. Rice flour in the banneton only hides the symptom [@breadcode-1006].

Two look-alikes are worth separating. A dough that flattens without tearing lacks strength rather than being over-fermented [@breadcode-1010]. And a slight spread out of the banneton is normal and usually recovers in the oven [@breadcode-2931].

A dough that can no longer be shaped can go into a greased loaf pan [@breadcode-1011]. Adding flour does not rescue it [@breadcode-1472]. Next time, shorten the bulk, lower the temperature, or make the starter less acidic with a larger feed ratio [@breadcode-2640].

## Gummy crumb: four causes

1. **Under-fermentation.** Gummy patches low in the loaf, with craters above [@breadcode-1048].
2. **Over-fermentation.** Gummy all through, with tiny alveoli: much of the crumb has been broken down [@breadcode-1030].
3. **Under-baking.** Go by the core temperature. The Framework takes loaves out at about 92 °C; several of the same author's videos and recipes use 95 °C [@breadcode-0660; @breadcode-1645; @breadcode-1162]. Very wet lean doughs can stay under-baked at the core [@novita-0374].
4. **Slicing too soon.** Cutting hot bread ruins the crumb and makes it gummy; let it cool to room temperature [@novita-0701].

A fifth cause applies to scalded doughs: above about 20 % of the flour in a tangzhong, Novita reports a risk of a sticky, gummy crumb [@novita-0371]. See [Tangzhong, yudane and other scalds](/en/learn/tangzhong-yudane-scalds).

## Large holes under the top crust

A row of big pockets just under the crust means the crust set before the loaf had finished expanding: the gas could no longer push outwards and the pockets merged beneath it. Too little steam does this, and so does an oven that is too hot [@breadcode-0672; @breadcode-0673]. A very dark crust together with pockets near the top points to too little steam [@breadcode-2249]. Without steam, the surface dries into a shell that limits the rise and tears where it can [@vanin2009].

Check the temperature first, then the steam [@breadcode-1071]. The oven display may not show the real temperature, so test it with a separate thermometer [@kleinwachter2021a]. A loaf baked from frozen shows the same pockets, because its crust sets while the centre is still frozen [@breadcode-2486]. [Baking and steam](/en/learn/baking-and-steam) covers the methods.

## A tight, even crumb

An even crumb of small alveoli is not a defect; it is what handling produces. Every touch degasses the dough and merges alveoli, so an open crumb asks for as little handling as possible [@breadcode-1019]. A tight preshape gives an even crumb, a minimal one a wild, open crumb [@breadcode-2232]. In one side-by-side bake at 85 % hydration, the preshaped loaf was the more regular and the directly shaped one the wilder [@breadcode-2696]. At shaping, a gentle pat keeps the large holes, and full degassing gives smaller ones [@novita-0204]. [Shaping and the final proof](/en/learn/shaping-and-final-proof) covers both steps.

The sources disagree on the gluten an open crumb needs. An early Bread Code guide asks for well-developed gluten, more water and folds right up to shaping [@breadcode-1127]. Novita asks for enough fermentation, more water, gentle handling and a weaker gluten network [@novita-0657]. They agree that the dough needs more water; [Mixing, rests and folds](/en/learn/mixing-and-gluten) covers how gluten is built.

## One change at a time

No recipe can be followed blindly: flour, tools and kitchen differ [@breadcode-0347]. Weigh everything and change a single parameter from one bake to the next, otherwise its effect cannot be read [@breadcode-1605]. Work in order: starter, then bulk, then proof [@breadcode-2984]. A small sample of dough in a marked jar shows when the bulk is done, whatever the clock says [@breadcode-2641]; [Bulk fermentation: knowing when to stop](/en/learn/bulk-fermentation-endpoints) explains how to use it.

Note the dose of starter, the dough temperature, the time and the rise for each bake. The [fermentation calculator](/en/calculators/fermentation) gives the expected time to compare with. For doughs that come out too sour, see [Sourdough flavour and acidity](/en/learn/sourdough-flavour-and-acidity).
