A hearth loaf is baked in two stages. The first, with steam, lets the dough expand; the second, without steam, builds the crust [@breadcode-0353].

## What happens in the oven

| Temperature | What happens |
|---|---|
| 55–65 °C | Starch gelatinizes and binds free water |
| About 60 °C | The yeasts and bacteria die |
| 70–80 °C | Gluten sets: the crumb takes its structure |
| About 75 °C, at the surface | A gel forms that stays extensible and holds gas |
| 100 °C | Water turns to steam and inflates the alveoli |
| 105–120 °C, once the surface is dry | Maillard browning starts |
| About 170 °C | Sugars caramelize |

The rows come from a published state diagram of bread processing and from *The Sourdough Framework* [@cuq2003; @breadcode-0648; @breadcode-0650; @breadcode-0651; @purlis2010; @breadcode-0658]. As long as the crumb holds water, the core does not exceed about 100 °C; only the drying crust goes higher [@breadcode-0993].

## Why steam, and how much

Steam condenses on the cool dough, heats its surface quickly to the gel stage and holds it near 100 °C, so the crust forms late and the loaf keeps expanding [@breadcode-0668; @breadcode-1072]. Without steam, the outer layer can pass 120 °C within five minutes [@lebail2011]. It dries into a shell that limits the rise and tears where it can; the stiffening comes mainly from drying, not from heat [@vanin2009].

How much steam is where the sources part.

- **"As much as possible."** This is the Framework's position, in the book and in the videos: a home oven vents steam, so the baker's problem is always too little [@breadcode-0695; @breadcode-1704].
- **Medium.** The studies that Novita Listyani summarizes are less simple. In one, loaf volume was largest with low or with high steam and smallest in between [@lebail2011]. In another, more steam gave a glossier and more brittle crust, but also a denser, less permeable one that traps the crumb's moisture and loses its crispness sooner in storage; its authors recommended a medium amount as the compromise [@altamiranofortoul2012]. She adds her own reading: heavy steam in a small vessel gives a voluminous, glossy loaf whose score closes without an ear [@novita-0094; @novita-0088].

The Framework's own tests show that too much is possible. With an apple as a stand-in for dough, the surface read 72 °C after 5 minutes with an ice cube in a preheated Dutch oven, 47 °C with a spritz of water, 43 °C in the plain preheated pot and 17 °C in a pot that was not preheated (apparently one run each) [@breadcode-0705]. With real dough, large ice cubes of about 80 g, four times the usual size, gave the author less oven spring [@breadcode-1076; @breadcode-1078]. In a video side-by-side, a normal ice cube gave more visible spring than spritzing, with no overall winner [@breadcode-2351].

In practice: an open home oven is a low-steam oven even with plenty of water; in a closed pot, a wet dough may need no added water and a stiffer one a small ice cube or a spray. The gloss of the crust tells you how much steam there was [@novita-0094]. Remove the steam for the second stage, because it softens the crust and blocks browning [@breadcode-0674].

## Vessels and methods

| Oven | Open oven, no tools | Inverted tray | Dutch oven |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gas | No | No | Yes |
| Fan that cannot be turned off | No | No | Yes |
| Fan that can be turned off | No | Yes | Yes |
| Steam oven | Yes | Yes | Yes |

The table is the Framework's [@breadcode-0699].

- **Dutch oven.** It is not sealed, but the water leaving the dough fills it with steam [@breadcode-0679]. Preheat it for 30 minutes at 230 °C [@breadcode-0680]: in the apple test, an unheated pot needed 26 minutes to bring the surface to 75 °C, against about 6 minutes for a preheated pot with an ice cube [@breadcode-0702; @breadcode-0700]. It bakes one loaf at a time and is heavy and dangerous when hot [@breadcode-0685; @breadcode-0687].
- **Inverted tray.** Loaves go on a preheated tray, a cold tray is turned upside down over them, and a bowl of boiling water supplies the steam; the fan must be off [@breadcode-0691; @breadcode-1069]. It takes several loaves at once.
- **Loaf pan.** Cover it with a second pan of the same size [@breadcode-0310].
- **Stone or steel.** It adds bottom heat, which helps the spring [@breadcode-2174]. Heat-storing vessels raise the heating rate, so preheat them well [@novita-0093].

## Temperatures

The Framework bakes free-standing loaves at about 230 °C: about 30 minutes with steam, then 10 to 30 minutes without, checked 5 minutes at a time, for colour [@breadcode-0676; @breadcode-0677; @breadcode-0684]. Its author calls 230 °C his sweet spot. Hotter, the crust closes early and the alveoli merge under it; too cold, a wet dough spreads before its starch sets [@breadcode-1067; @breadcode-1064; @breadcode-1066]. Pan loaves bake at about 200 °C, because their edges heat slowly and the top would overcook first [@breadcode-0308; @breadcode-0309].

Novita starts hotter: a covered pot at 250 °C for 15 to 25 minutes, then 210–230 °C uncovered [@novita-0377; @novita-0615]. For sugar-rich doughs she drops to about 170 °C, since they brown fast, and covers the top with paper if it darkens early [@novita-0326]; [Enriched doughs](/en/learn/enriched-doughs) covers those bakes. Both can be right for their ovens: the display can differ from the real temperature, so check yours with a thermometer [@kleinwachter2021a].

## Done: the core temperature

Crust colour is a poor guide, especially in a pan: one pan loaf that looked ready was still underdone at 84 °C [@breadcode-2643]. A probe in the thickest part is the reliable check, but the sources give different numbers.

- The Framework: about 92 °C, for hearth, pan and rye loaves alike [@breadcode-0660; @breadcode-0307; @breadcode-0340].
- The same author's recipes and several of his videos: 95 °C [@breadcode-1162; @breadcode-1645].
- Novita: getting a very wet lean dough above 90 °C at the core is the difficulty; she bakes hot first, then lower and longer [@novita-0374].

At the target, the bread is cooked but the crust may not be built; keep baking for colour only [@breadcode-0661]. A pale crust is the safer error, since a loaf can go back into the oven later [@breadcode-0659]. A hollow knock is only a secondary sign [@breadcode-1108].

## Bake loss

A loaf weighs less than its dough. Novita puts the water lost in the oven at about 10–20 % of the dough weight, a figure she gives without a source [@novita-0689]. Measure your own: weigh the dough going in and the loaf once cooled; the loss is the difference divided by the dough weight. The [formula calculator](/en/calculators/formula) takes a bake loss, so that the dough is scaled for the loaf weight you want.

## Cooling before slicing

A hot crumb is fragile. Cut too soon, it tears, sticks to the knife and turns gummy [@breadcode-1705; @breadcode-2591; @novita-0701]. The sources ask for 30 minutes at the very least and more often 1 to 2 hours, on a rack so that the base does not go soggy [@breadcode-1649; @breadcode-2250; @breadcode-1238]. Taking the loaf straight out into cool air also helps the crust: fine cracks open as it cools and vent moisture, and a cracked crust stays crisp longer [@hirte2013].

For keeping bread after that, see [Storing and freezing bread](/en/learn/storing-and-freezing-bread); for holes under the crust and gummy crumb, [Troubleshooting the crumb](/en/learn/troubleshooting-crumb).
