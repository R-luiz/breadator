Bread ages in three ways: the crumb loses water, its starch slowly firms up, and mould arrives [@breadcode-0769; @yamauchi2014; @breadcode-0781]. No storage method stops all three, so each one trades the crust against the crumb, or texture against time.

| Storage | Keeps for | The price |
|---|---|---|
| Room temperature, uncovered | about a day | dries out fast |
| Room temperature, airtight | up to a week | soft crust, mould sooner |
| Fridge, airtight | weeks | needs toasting |
| Freezer, sliced | long term | thaw, then toast |

The table summarizes Table 11.1 of *The Sourdough Framework* [@breadcode-0794; @breadcode-0791; @breadcode-0792; @breadcode-0793]. The fridge line is disputed; see below.

## Why bread goes stale

**Water.** A loaf left out keeps losing water from its crumb until it is hard [@breadcode-0769]. The crust turns chewy first, after about a day, as the moisture left in the bread evens out between crust and crumb and partly evaporates; in humid tropical air a crust may stay crisp for only a few hours [@breadcode-1014; @breadcode-1015].

**Starch.** The starch gelatinized in the oven slowly returns to an ordered, firmer state: this is retrogradation. It depends on the water present while the bread ages, and a moister crumb is softer and firms more slowly [@yamauchi2014; @novita-0650].

What slows it follows from this:

- **More water.** By the Framework's figures, a low-hydration loaf dries out in 1–2 days at room temperature and a high-hydration loaf in 3–4 [@breadcode-0771; @breadcode-0772].
- **A scald.** Gelatinized flour holds water; a lean dough has no sugar or fat to slow staling and is otherwise best eaten the same day [@novita-0579; @novita-0303]. See [Tangzhong, yudane and other scalds](/en/learn/tangzhong-yudane-scalds).
- **Some fat.** About 6 % butter softens the crumb and helps it last [@novita-0168]; see [Enriched doughs](/en/learn/enriched-doughs).
- **Less yeast.** In Calvel's view, a large dose of baker's yeast shortens the fermentation and gives a bread that stales faster [@calvel2001].

**Mould** is a separate matter. Acidity holds it back, which is why sourdough bread keeps longer in a container than yeast bread [@breadcode-0781]. In one study of salt-reduced bread, a sourdough made with a strain chosen for its antifungal effect gave a shelf life against mould of 12–14 days, against 10–12 days with 0.3 % calcium propionate; a home starter may do less [@belz2012]. Slower mould does not mean slower drying: a Bread Code video notes that a sourdough loaf dries out rather quickly [@breadcode-1647]. A loaf with visible mould is thrown away whole, not trimmed: bread is porous, and the threads and toxins of a mould can reach beyond the spot you see [@usdepartmentofagriculture2013; @bfr2026].

## At room temperature

Cut side down on a board, a loaf is at its best within about a day, and its crust stays crisp as long as the air is not humid [@breadcode-0767]. A paper bag or a bread box is not sealed, so the bread still dries a little [@breadcode-0776]. A sealed plastic bag keeps the moisture in and the bread soft for up to a week, but that moisture moves into the crust, which goes leathery, and mould comes sooner; toasting restores the crispness [@breadcode-0777; @breadcode-0791; @breadcode-0778].

The crust itself can be helped at the bake. A crust loses its crispness once its moisture passes about 10 %; a thin, permeable crust lets water out, a dense one traps it [@altamiranofortoul2012]. Fine cracks that open while the loaf cools on a rack act as vents, and in one study such crusts stayed crisp much longer in the first hours [@hirte2013]. [Baking and steam](/en/learn/baking-and-steam) explains the role of steam.

## The fridge: the sources disagree

The Framework's author stores bread in a sealed container in the fridge. In his experience it does not dry out quickly and keeps for weeks without mould, where the same sealed bread would mould at room temperature; it needs toasting afterwards [@breadcode-0782; @breadcode-0785; @breadcode-0786]. He notes that other sources say the opposite: that bread dries out in the fridge, and that liquid is drawn from the crumb to the surface [@breadcode-0783; @breadcode-0784].

One of his own videos is more reserved: about a week in an airtight bag, with the mould held back but the crumb drying out quickly [@breadcode-1646]. Novita Listyani describes the mechanism on scalds: gelatinized starch retrogrades at fridge temperature, around 4 °C, and releases water [@novita-0727]. None of our sources measures the same bread at room temperature and in the fridge. What they agree on is the division of labour: the fridge buys time against mould, costs texture, and reheating makes the bread taste fresh again [@breadcode-2924].

## The freezer

For long keeping, freeze. The Framework slices the whole loaf and freezes the slices in portions that will each be eaten within a day [@breadcode-0787]. Thaw them for a few hours, then toast or bake them to the crispness you want [@breadcode-0788]. Novita freezes her whole-wheat loaves and reheats them in the oven or microwave after a spritz of water, so that they do not dry out [@novita-0030].

The Framework also cites a study in which white bread that had been frozen and then toasted gave a blood sugar response almost 40 % lower than fresh bread [@breadcode-0790; @burton2007]. It is one study on one kind of bread, not a figure to apply to every loaf.

## Reheating

- **A dried-out loaf.** Hold it under the tap for 10–15 seconds and bake it again [@breadcode-0773].
- **With steam.** Reheat at about 150 °C with a tray of boiling water in the oven; the steam keeps the crust from thickening. Without water the crust gets crisper and the crumb drier [@breadcode-1130; @breadcode-2924].
- **Par-baking.** Bake with steam until the core reaches at least 95 °C, leaving the crust to be finished later; cool for at least 2 hours, bag and refrigerate; finish for 10–15 minutes at 230 °C [@breadcode-1675; @breadcode-2194; @breadcode-1311; @breadcode-2051]. The Bread Code's author says par-baked rolls keep up to 7 days at room temperature [@breadcode-2051].
- **Too far gone.** Dry bread makes breadcrumbs [@breadcode-0775].

## Freezing unbaked dough

Freezing hurts dough. Part of the yeast dies, ice crystals pull water out of the gluten, and the dead yeast releases glutathione, which weakens the gluten further. The bread is smaller and firmer, the more so the longer the storage and the slower the freezing [@novita-0139].

On when to freeze, the two creators go opposite ways.

- **Before fermentation.** From the literature she summarizes, Novita freezes dough before it has fermented, keeps it under about a month and avoids thawing and refreezing [@novita-0144]. She uses a higher-protein flour and some solid fat, and notes that 6–8 % honey protected the gluten [@novita-0141; @novita-0140]. A freeze-tolerant yeast is best; failing that, an osmotolerant one, in a slightly higher dose [@novita-0143].
- **After the proof.** The Bread Code froze a shaped sourdough loaf once its proof was nearly complete, and baked it a week later straight from the freezer: about 40 minutes at 230 °C to reach 94 °C at the core [@breadcode-2480; @breadcode-2481; @breadcode-2484]. It worked, with less oven spring and large pockets, because the crust set while the centre was still frozen [@breadcode-2486]. This was a single test.

Frozen pizza dough should thaw in the fridge, in about a day, never at room temperature [@breadcode-1184].
