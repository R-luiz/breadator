Inoculation is the amount of ripe starter in a dough, relative to its flour. It decides how long the bulk fermentation takes, and how much acid and already-fermented flour the dough starts with. This article counts it as *The Sourdough Framework* does: weight of starter as a percentage of the flour weight. The other convention counts the flour inside the starter (prefermented flour); 20 g of a 100 % hydration starter brings 10 g of flour and 10 g of water. Either way, that flour and water belong in the dough totals, or the hydration is miscalculated [@novita-0138]. The [formula calculator](/en/calculators/formula) shows both conventions, and [Baker's percentages](/en/learn/bakers-percentages) explains how to count them.

## Table 7.1: dose against temperature

| Temperature | Recently fed starter | Starving starter |
|---|---|---|
| 30 °C | 5 % | 2.5 % |
| 25 °C | 10 % | 5 % |
| 20 °C | 15 % | 10 % |

These are the Framework's figures, starter weight on flour weight [@science-ferm-0054]. The table states no duration; the text around it aims for 8 to 12 hours of bulk [@science-ferm-0053]. Run through a growth model with a reference starter, the three "recently fed" rows reach a 50 % rise in about 9, 8 and 12 hours, so the table does sit in that window [@science-ferm-0055].

The book and its author's videos do not always agree with the table. The prose of the same chapter gives about 5 % at about 25 °C in summer, half the table's value, and 10–20 % at about 20 °C in winter [@breadcode-0433; @breadcode-0434]. A recent video gives 10 % as the base, 2.5–5 % in a kitchen near 30 °C and never more than 20 % [@breadcode-2927; @breadcode-2928; @breadcode-2929]; an earlier one uses 10 % in summer and 20 % in winter for the same 8–12 hours [@breadcode-1380]. Read the table as a range to start from, not as a setting.

## Low or high: two schools

The Framework argues for small doses. Most recipes call for about 20 %; its author prefers 5–10 % [@breadcode-0423]. A small dose lets the culture regrow in the dough into a balanced state, which forgives a starter that is not at its best [@breadcode-0435]. A large one brings more prefermented flour, in which enzymes have already done their work, and a long fermentation then ends in a sticky dough [@breadcode-0430].

Novita Listyani works at the other end. Her dough is 1 part levain to 5 parts flour and 4 parts water, that is 20 % [@novita-0643], and the reviews she summarizes describe household sourdough as having 20–30 % of its flour prefermented [@novita-0147]. These are two schedules more than two truths: a small dose for a long, often overnight bulk; a large one for a shorter day.

## What the dose does to the clock

In one practitioner's series of more than 60 test doughs (2 % salt, starter used near its peak), a 75 % rise took 12 hours at 20 °C and 8.5 hours at 23 °C with 20 % starter, and 18 and 14 hours with 5 % [@science-ferm-0041]. Four times less starter made the bulk 1.5 to 1.7 times as long, not four times as long, because the culture multiplies in the dough. The same baker reckons that going from 20 % to 10 % lengthens the bulk by about a third [@science-ferm-0047].

Starters differ as much as doses do. Bill Wraith's table predicts a bulk of 3 h 40 at 24 °C with 20 % inoculation [@science-ferm-0048]; the Bread Code's author reports about 10 hours at about 22 °C with the same dose, where that table says 6.5 [@science-ferm-0051]. Across six sources, the speed of the starter runs from about half to 1.6 times the reference [@science-ferm-0052].

## Fresh or starving starter

The older the starter, the smaller the dose. The Framework goes up to 20 % for a starter fed only 6–8 hours earlier and down to about 1 % (10 g per kilogram of flour) for one fed about 48 hours earlier [@breadcode-0178; @breadcode-0177]. The videos give about 5 % for a starter kept unfed in the fridge and about 1 % for one unfed for a week [@breadcode-2058; @breadcode-2213]. The reason: as an unfed starter turns more acidic, its yeast fades and its bacteria dominate, and a large dose of it gives a sour, weak dough [@breadcode-2218]. At about 1 %, a flatbread dough is ready in about 24 hours, depending on temperature [@breadcode-0291].

There is a limit. After a very long time without food the microbes go dormant, and they may wake too slowly to ferment the dough before the flour's proteases break down the gluten [@breadcode-0967]. In a single experiment, a dough made directly from a starter left 30 days at room temperature tore completely after about 24 hours [@breadcode-0966]. A neglected starter needs feeding first; see [Maintaining a starter](/en/learn/starter-maintenance).

## Building a levain from a seed

A levain build is a feed sized for the recipe: you keep a small starter, take a seed from it and feed that seed up to the amount the dough needs. For a ratio 1:f:w (seed:flour:water), the seed is the levain weight divided by 1 + f + w. The [starter feeding calculator](/en/calculators/starter) does this sum for the amount you need, and gives the time to peak at your temperature or the ratio that peaks when you want.

| Levain needed | Ratio | Seed | Flour | Water |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 g | 1:2:2 | 20 g | 40 g | 40 g |
| 110 g | 1:5:5 | 10 g | 50 g | 50 g |

The first row is Novita's levain, the second the Framework's feed [@novita-0729; @breadcode-0359]. Novita uses hers at its peak, 3–4 hours at 27–30 °C or overnight in the fridge [@novita-0381]. By her arithmetic, 20 g of seed built this way, risen to at least double in 5–6 hours at room temperature, does the work of about 100 g of starter used directly, and it lets you keep a small starter [@novita-0729]. She adds that the acidity of a sour levain inhibits amylase, which is why she uses it at its peak [@novita-0383]. The Framework makes no separate step of it: its 1:5:5 feed, 6–12 hours before mixing, is the build [@breadcode-0357].

One more reason to build rather than drop old starter into the dough: salt. Above about 0.7 %, it suppresses the lactic acid bacteria much more strongly than the yeast, so Novita advises letting the culture grow in an unsalted levain first [@simonson2003; @novita-0736].

## Adjusting to your kitchen

- **Temperature first.** Blended from published growth curves, the rate relative to 25 °C is about 0.6 at 20 °C and 0.27 at 15 °C, and only about 1.15 at 28–30 °C before it falls again [@science-ferm-0040]. In a cool kitchen, raise the dose (Table 7.1 asks 15 % at 20 °C) or mix with warmer water [@breadcode-2677]; the [water temperature calculator](/en/calculators/temperature) gives the water temperature for a target dough temperature, and [Dough temperature](/en/learn/dough-temperature) explains the method. In great heat, the Framework goes as low as about 1 % [@breadcode-0462].
- **Flour.** In the Framework author's own bakes, whole-wheat dough over-fermented quickly, and using less starter was part of the fix [@breadcode-0041].
- **Your starter.** Without calibration, the model's 80 % range for a bulk time is a factor of about 1.7 either way; after three logged bakes it narrows to about 1.3 [@science-ferm-0079]. Write down dose, dough temperature, time and result.

The [fermentation calculator](/en/calculators/fermentation) gives the time for a dose, or the dose for a time, at your dough temperature; [Fermentation time](/en/learn/fermentation-time-and-temperature) explains how temperature, dose and endpoint combine.
