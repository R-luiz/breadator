A sourdough starter is flour and water in which wild yeasts and lactic acid bacteria have multiplied until they reliably ferment fresh flour. The yeasts make most of the gas that raises bread; the bacteria make the acids that give it its tang and keep it from spoiling [@breadcode-0066; @breadcode-0001]. Bacteria outnumber yeasts by roughly a hundred to one, although real starters vary widely [@breadcode-0065; @minervini2012; @breadcode-1593]. [Sourdough flavour and acidity](/en/learn/sourdough-flavour-and-acidity) covers the two acids.

## What lives in the jar

Two kinds of lactic acid bacteria matter. Homofermentative strains make only lactic acid; heterofermentative strains make lactic acid plus acetic acid, ethanol and some carbon dioxide [@breadcode-0070; @breadcode-0071]. Below about 30 °C a starter tends to favour the heterofermentative group [@novita-0233; @ercolini2013].

Most of the microbes arrive with the flour; hands and air play a minor part [@breadcode-0094; @breadcode-0105]. In the first days, yeasts, bacteria, moulds and other germs all wake up and compete. The winners are those that eat glucose and maltose fastest or live on the others' waste, and the acid they make keeps newcomers out [@breadcode-0100; @breadcode-0103; @breadcode-0141]. Each feeding selects microbes that are better at fermenting the flour you use, which is why a starter keeps improving after it first rises [@calvertmd2021].

## Starting one

- **Flour.** Use a whole-grain flour (whole wheat, rye, spelt): the microbes live on the outer layers of the grain, so whole flour carries more of them and starts faster [@breadcode-0093; @breadcode-2156]. A starter made only from white flour sometimes fails [@breadcode-0912]. Whole gluten-free flours such as rice or corn also work [@breadcode-0095].
- **Water.** Chlorine is a disinfectant and slows the microbes. Bottled water is the safe choice; tap water is fine in some regions [@breadcode-0097]. Novita Listyani boils chlorinated tap water for 15 minutes or leaves it to stand for 24 hours [@novita-0395].
- **Amounts.** About 50 g flour and 50 g water (100 % hydration) is enough; the exact numbers are only a reference [@breadcode-0096]. Cover loosely so gas can escape, for example with an upturned glass [@breadcode-0099].
- **Warmth.** The Bread Code's starter recipe uses water between 20 and 30 °C [@breadcode-1143]. Novita says a starter needs warm conditions, above about 25 °C, to get going [@novita-0206]. *The Sourdough Framework* adds an untested idea: a cooler start may select cold-tolerant microbes and a different flavour [@breadcode-0087].

## What to expect, day by day

| When | What you see | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | A paste | Mix, cover loosely |
| About 24 h | A slight smell, a few bubbles, maybe a small rise | Keep 10 g, discard the rest, feed 50 g flour and 50 g water |
| Days 2–3 | Activity may fade | Keep feeding every 24 h |
| Day 4 onward | Rise, bubbles, a smell of vinegar or yoghurt | Keep feeding until it is reliably active |

The table follows *The Sourdough Framework* [@breadcode-0108; @breadcode-0109; @breadcode-0119; @breadcode-0120; @breadcode-0124]. Throw away the day-one leftovers rather than baking with them: they hold activated microbes that may be harmful [@breadcode-0115].

The quiet spell on days 2–3 is normal. A few microbes are taking over, and the activity continues out of sight [@breadcode-0119]. How long the whole process takes depends on the flour's microbes: about 4 days for some bakers, 7 for others, up to 20 [@breadcode-0121]. Novita gives 5–7 days [@novita-0236]; the Bread Code videos say about 7 days, sometimes up to 15 [@breadcode-1490]. If nothing happens after more than ten feedings, start again, ideally with another batch or another kind of flour [@breadcode-0132; @breadcode-2096]. Feeding for one extra day after it looks ready pays off later [@breadcode-1437].

**Ready** means three signs together: it has grown, it is full of bubbles and pockets, and it smells of vinegar or yoghurt [@breadcode-0124; @breadcode-0129]. A common time benchmark is doubling within 5–10 hours of a feed at room temperature [@breadcode-1490].

### The feeding ratio: the sources disagree

*The Sourdough Framework*'s start-up routine feeds 1 part starter to 5 parts flour and 5 parts water (1:5:5) from the first feed, and its general advice is to avoid 1:1:1 because no dough is ever mixed that way [@breadcode-0109; @breadcode-0110; @breadcode-0365]; its one exception is a new starter, where 1:1:1 speeds up the early days while microbe numbers are low [@breadcode-0900]. The author's own earlier recipe and a later video feed 1:1:1 daily until the starter doubles within about 10 hours [@breadcode-1144; @breadcode-2957]. Novita also maintains at 1:1:1 and feeds every 12–24 hours [@novita-0208; @novita-0209]. A 1:5:5 feed leaves less acid behind; a 1:1:1 feed peaks sooner [@breadcode-2338; @breadcode-0369]. Peak times by ratio and temperature are in [Maintaining a starter](/en/learn/starter-maintenance), and the [starter feeding calculator](/en/calculators/starter) gives them for your own ratio and temperature.

## Myths that make people give up

- **"It died on day 3."** Usually it has not: the lull is the selection phase described above [@breadcode-0119]. Mature starters are very hard to kill. When food runs out, the microbes form spores that wait for the next feed [@breadcode-0143; @breadcode-0146].
- **"It must double, or triple."** There is no fixed rise. It depends on the microbes and the flour [@breadcode-0126]. Rye holds little gas, so a healthy rye starter may barely rise [@breadcode-0889].
- **"It must pass the float test."** The Framework's author does not recommend it: active rye starters often sink, and low-gluten flours do too [@breadcode-0133; @breadcode-2032]. Novita uses the float test, yet in one of her tests a sample from the top floated before the starter was ready [@novita-0211; @novita-0455].
- **"Liquid on top means it is ruined."** That layer (hooch) means the starter is hungry. It is harmless, even protects against mould, and can be stirred back in [@breadcode-0848; @breadcode-0921].
- **"An acetone smell means it went off."** It comes from acetic acid; feeding fixes it [@breadcode-0128; @breadcode-1984; @breadcode-1148].
- **"Older is always better."** Maturity helps [@breadcode-1692], but how actively a starter rises after a feed predicts the bake better than its age [@breadcode-1433]. A gifted mature starter may be stronger at first; a new one catches up [@breadcode-0122].
- **"Fruit, kefir or kombucha make a better start."** Cultures from those may not ferment flour well [@breadcode-2025]. A fruit-based starter can lack the acid-making bacteria, and its pH should fall below about 4.2 [@breadcode-0235; @breadcode-0236].

## Mould and odd colours

Young starters can grow mould, because every flour carries mould spores that compete with the yeasts and bacteria [@breadcode-0926]. A mature starter rarely moulds unless conditions change, for example when its surface dries out in the fridge [@breadcode-0928]. The Framework's remedy is to take 1–2 g from a part with no mould and feed it a few times [@breadcode-0930]. Food-safety agencies say otherwise about moist, porous foods: the mould you see is only part of the organism, its threads and toxins can reach below the surface, and such foods are thrown away [@usdepartmentofagriculture2013; @bfr2026]. Neither agency mentions starters; by their reasoning the cautious choice is to discard a mouldy starter and begin a new one [@science-bread-0103].

Novita warns about pink or red colouring from *Serratia marcescens*, an unwanted bacterium, and adds an acidic liquid such as yoghurt or honey water at the start to lower the pH early [@novita-0207].
