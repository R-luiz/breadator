A grain is a seed, and water wakes the enzymes it stores for the sprout; milled flour still holds them [@breadcode-0016; @breadcode-0017]. Two matter most to the baker: amylase, which cuts starch into sugars, and protease, which cuts proteins, gluten included [@breadcode-0021]. Both work faster in a warm dough [@breadcode-1089] and, with the microbes, set the softness of the crumb, the colour of the crust and how much gluten survives [@breadcode-0046].

## Amylases: starch into sugar

α-Amylase cuts starch into shorter fragments, giving maltose and some glucose; β-amylase turns those fragments into maltose [@breadcode-0801; @breadcode-0821]. Maltose feeds the yeast [@breadcode-0854], and the sugars left at the end brown the crust [@breadcode-0025]. α-Amylase only attacks damaged or gelatinized starch [@bakerpedianda]. Sound wheat flour has considerable β-amylase but little α-amylase, unless the grain sprouted before harvest or carries late-maturity amylase [@rebholz2020].

## The oven window

In a baking loaf (see [baking and steam](/en/learn/baking-and-steam)), IREKS puts wheat starch gelatinization at 60 °C to 78–89 °C and rye starch at 50 °C to 65–76 °C, and inactivates β-amylase at 60 °C, α-amylase at 75 °C and the flour's enzymes above 80 °C [@gmbhnd]. With the rule that α-amylase needs gelatinized or damaged starch, this suggests a short window, from about 60 °C (wheat starch starts to gelatinize) to 75 °C (α-amylase inactivated), in which it has fresh starch to cut. That is this article's own inference, neither stated by the sources nor measured [@bakerpedianda; @gmbhnd].

Other sources give other figures. Novita Listyani has β-amylase best at 60–65 °C and inactivated above 70 °C, α-amylase best at 70–75 °C and quickly inactivated at 80 °C [@novita-0118]. The end point was measured in one study: a pan white bread baked 35 minutes at 230 °C peaked at 97 °C in the crumb, and the amylases of the flour and the yeast were fully inactivated [@reichenberger2020]. Scald temperatures are covered in [tangzhong, yudane and other scalds](/en/learn/tangzhong-yudane-scalds).

## The falling number

The falling number measures α-amylase indirectly: it is the time in seconds, 60 s of stirring included, for a stirrer to sink through a paste of 7 g flour and 25 mL water heated in a boiling-water bath. More α-amylase thins the paste and lowers the number [@gmbhnd]. Added fungal amylase does not register [@bakerpedianda].

| Flour | High activity | Normal | Low activity | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wheat, German type 550 | 240–300 s | 300–360 s | 360–420 s | [@gmbhnd] |
| Rye, types 997 and 1150 | 120–180 s | 180–240 s | 240–300 s | [@gmbhnd] |

These are IREKS's classes; other sources do not use them. BAKERpedia says falling numbers above 250 s suit bread baking, most large bakeries aim for 250–280 s and above 350 s the flour may need amylase or malt, the right value depending on product and plant [@bakerpedianda]. For high-quality rye bread, 125–200 s is given, and for rye the number describes the starch's properties more than enzyme activity alone [@murniece2024].

For IREKS, high activity means a faster, slightly damper wheat dough with stronger colour, and low activity a weak proof and a small, pale loaf [@gmbhnd]. A low number from late-maturity amylase is not proof of poor baking: at 157–294 s, wheat lines showed no consistent link with oven spring, loaf volume or crumb firmness [@newberry2018].

## Malt and added amylase

Diastatic malt is dried, ground malted grain whose enzymes still work; non-diastatic malt is dried hotter, which deactivates them, and brings only flavour and colour [@breadcode-0829; @breadcode-0855]. A study of rye malt gives recommended additions of 0.2 to 2 %, depending on diastatic power and flour quality [@murniece2024]. Too much softens the dough, can make the loaf's sides cave in and, in long fermentations, leaves dough and crumb sticky [@bakerpedianda]. In one wheat-bread study, three fungal α-amylases left no activity after baking, but a bacterial one kept 4 % and was still producing short sugars in the crumb after 96 hours [@rebholz2020].

## Rye: why it needs acid

Rye has more α-amylase than wheat, and its starch gelatinizes at lower temperatures, overlapping the enzyme's best range. Unchecked, the enzyme breaks down the starch framework, which then cannot hold the dough water: the crumb turns damp and the crust can come away [@gmbhnd]. IREKS puts the α-amylase optimum at pH 5–5.5 (BAKERpedia at 5.5–6.0), and sourdough acid holds it back [@gmbhnd; @bakerpedianda]. IREKS's guide values for rye bread doughs are pH 4.2–4.5 with a degree of acidity (the German TTA) of 7–10, against pH 5.2–5.6 and 4–5 for wheat [@gmbhnd]. In one IREKS example, a type 1150 rye with a falling number of about 120 s gave a stable, sliceable crumb only from 5 % of a commercial acidifier (degree of acidity 200) on the flour; that is a single demonstration with one flour and one product, not a dose rule [@gmbhnd]. Today's hybrid rye, poorer in enzymes, more often fails through small volume and dry crumb and needs less acid [@gmbhnd].

## Proteases and the slack dough

Protease cuts proteins into peptides and amino acids: moderate activity makes a dough extensible, too much leaves it slack and sticky, with poor volume [@breadcode-0870]. It works more with time and warmth, acidity activates it, and flour from sprouted or malted grain carries more [@breadcode-0871; @breadcode-0872]. A 15–30-minute autolyse leaves it little time [@novita-0105], whereas a long fermentation lets it make the dough easier to inflate [@breadcode-0037]. Too long a rest and the dough loses its structure: with his flour, The Bread Code's author saw the limit at about 12 hours at 22 °C and 10 hours at 25 °C, a personal observation [@breadcode-1803; @breadcode-1881]. Novita Listyani says salt brakes proteolysis (see [salt in bread](/en/learn/salt-in-bread)) [@novita-0053]. Rests are covered in [mixing, rests and folds](/en/learn/mixing-and-gluten).

## Phytase

Cereal phytase works best at pH 4.3–5.5 [@ribet2026]. In vitro, a whole-wheat dough brought to about pH 5.5, by sourdough or by lactic acid alone, lost 70 % of its phytate against 40 % with neither leavening nor acid, mostly through the wheat's own phytase [@leenhardt2004]; whole-wheat bread lost 62 % with sourdough and 38 % with yeast [@lopez2001]. For iron and minerals, see [bread, fermentation and health](/en/learn/bread-and-nutrition).
