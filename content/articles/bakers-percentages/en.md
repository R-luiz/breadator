Baker's math writes every ingredient as a percentage of the flour weight, with flour as the 100 % baseline [@breadcode-0089]. A dough of 1,000 g flour, 600 g water, 100 g starter and 20 g salt reads 100 / 60 / 10 / 2 [@breadcode-0090]. The percentages do not add up to 100, and they are not meant to: they are proportions. The same formula gives 1,400 g flour, 840 g water, 140 g starter and 28 g salt, or any other batch, without rewriting the recipe [@breadcode-0091]. A French professional training site writes the same thing in grams per kilogram of flour: its 10 to 24 g of fresh yeast per kg is 1 to 2.4 % [@science-ferm-0013].

## Total formula and stages

A levain, a poolish or a scald is not an ingredient of its own. It is flour and water of the bread, prepared earlier, so its flour and water belong in the totals; leaving them out gives a wrong hydration [@novita-0138]. The total formula describes the whole bread with all the flour at 100 %. Each stage takes its share out of those totals, and the final mix is what remains. Converting a recipe to a [tangzhong](/en/learn/tangzhong-yudane-scalds) shows the logic: choose the share of the flour to scald, move it into the scald with one to two times its weight of the recipe's water, and the totals stay the same [@novita-0128].

Many recipes are written the other way round, with the starter as one line among the others. Both views are valid, but they give different numbers for the same dough. With a starter at 100 % hydration, the example above holds 1,050 g flour and 650 g water: 61.9 % hydration, not 60 %. A larger dose widens the gap: 400 g flour, 260 g water and 160 g starter is 65 % water on the added flour and about 70 % once the starter's 80 g of flour and 80 g of water are counted [@breadcode-1924].

## Prefermented flour

The share of the total flour that ferments before the final mix is the prefermented flour; [preferments](/en/learn/preferments) describes the types. It compares a levain, a poolish and a biga whatever their hydration. Household sourdough breads typically preferment about 20–30 % of the flour [@novita-0147], and a yeasted preferment can hold up to 50 % [@novita-0597]. Check the basis of any figure you read: a poolish "at 20 %" in one recipe means 100 g of poolish for 500 g of flour. With the 500 g added at the final mix, the poolish's 50 g of flour are 50 g of 550 g, or 9.1 % prefermented flour [@breadcode-2798]. More prefermented flour brings more enzyme-worked flour into the dough, which turns sticky if fermentation then runs long [@breadcode-0430].

## Inoculation: two conventions, and a third

"20 % starter" usually means starter weight over the flour added at the final mix. *The Sourdough Framework* counts that way: its example puts 100 g of starter on 1,000 g of flour, 10 % [@breadcode-0090], and its Table 7.1 gives, for a recently fed starter, 5 % at 30 °C, 10 % at 25 °C and 15 % at 20 °C [@science-ferm-0054]; [levain and inoculation](/en/learn/levain-and-inoculation) reads that table in full. A second convention divides by the total flour, the starter's flour included. Fermentation models use a third number, the share of the total flour that comes from the starter, which is the prefermented flour again [@science-ferm-0043].

| 100 g of starter at 100 % hydration in 500 g of flour | Value |
|---|---|
| Starter / final-mix flour | 20 % |
| Starter / total flour (550 g) | 18.2 % |
| Starter flour / total flour | 9.1 % |

A stiff starter carries more flour per gram: the same 100 g at 55 % hydration brings 64.5 g of flour, 11.4 % of the total. Bill Wraith's rise-time table is indexed by this third number: its 4.8 % column is a dough with 10 % of starter at 100 % hydration [@science-ferm-0048]. When you copy a dose from one source to another, check which of the three it is.

## Hydration: which water counts

Hydration is water weight over flour weight: 60 % means 60 g of water per 100 g of flour [@breadcode-2884]. In [enriched doughs](/en/learn/enriched-doughs) other ingredients bring water. Butter contains some, to be counted when you calculate a formula [@novita-0855]. Eggs are mostly water, about 90 % for the white and 50 % for the yolk, and their size varies, so weigh them [@novita-0799; @novita-0800]. Milk is mostly water as well, and milk powder stands in for it at 1 g of powder to 7.5 g of water [@novita-0398].

Two hydration figures are therefore worth keeping. Water alone compares the dough with lean recipes; the water of all liquids says how the dough will handle. One whole-wheat loaf made with milk shows the difference: 250 g of water on 375 g of flour is 66.7 %, and the recipe states 77 % because it adds the water of its 45 g of milk [@novita-0034]. When a recipe quotes a hydration, find out which one.

## Dough yield

Some sourdough studies quote dough yield instead: the weight of dough obtained from 100 parts of flour, that is 100 plus the hydration. A study of four traditional sourdoughs kept them firm at dough yield 160, which is 60 % hydration, or liquid at 280, which is 180 % [@dicagnor2014].

## Scaling from the bread you want

Add up the percentages. The example formula totals 172 %, so 172 g of dough holds 100 g of flour, and the flour for any dough weight is that weight × 100 / 172. Every other ingredient follows from its percentage [@breadcode-0388]. For a pan, one creator multiplies the pan volume by a dough density (257–286 g of dough per litre in her lidded pan) and scales the flour to that weight [@novita-0230; @novita-0478].

Starting from the baked weight needs one more number. Water leaves the dough as steam in the oven [@breadcode-0814], so a loaf weighs less than its dough piece. Bake loss is that difference as a percentage of the dough weight, and dough weight = baked weight / (1 − loss). A flatter, wider loaf with more surface bakes faster and is reported to lose more water than a compact one [@novita-0656; @estevezlopez2021], so no single figure fits every bread; [baking and steam](/en/learn/baking-and-steam) gives the usual range. Weigh a dough piece before baking and the loaf once it has cooled, and use your own number. If your loaves lose 15 %, two loaves of 800 g need 1,600 / 0.85 = 1,882 g of dough, which is 1,094 g of flour with the 172 % formula.
