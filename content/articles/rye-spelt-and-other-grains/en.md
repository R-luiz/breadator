Doughs made mostly of bread wheat or spelt can be kneaded, folded and shaped; rye, emmer, einkorn, rice and corn doughs are simply mixed until even, according to one sourdough handbook [@science-flour-0044]. Spelt, einkorn, emmer, khorasan and durum are all wheat (*Triticum*) in EU law, and with rye and barley they are gluten cereals [@science-flour-0046]: none of them is suitable for people with coeliac disease, and sourdough fermentation does not make their bread safe [@science-bread-0137; @science-bread-0133]. For wheat flour labels, see [flour types](/en/learn/flour-types).

## Rye

**Types.** French rye flours are T70, T85, T130 and T170, with overlapping ash ranges and no stated basis [@science-flour-0003]. German rye runs from 815 to 1740, plus 1800, a coarse meal without the germ [@science-flour-0012]; 1370 and 1740 are the typical bakers' flours for rye breads, 997 and 1150 for mixed breads [@science-flour-0101]. American "dark rye" is a clear flour, the stream left after the white flour is taken, not a wholemeal [@science-flour-0102].

**No gluten network.** Rye proteins form no gluten network, and its pentosans (arabinoxylans) are the main water-binding polymers of the dough, governing its rheology and gas retention [@science-flour-0098]; they also keep gluten bonds from forming, so the crumb stays dense [@buksa2010]. German practice puts a 100 % rye bread at 82–86 % water against 60–62 % for wheat bread [@science-flour-0030].

**Amylase and acid.** Rye flour has more α-amylase than wheat, and its starch gelatinizes at a lower temperature [@science-bread-0022]: a wholemeal rye flour started at 54.6 °C on an amylograph, a white wheat flour at 56.1 °C, in one study [@wojciechowiczbudzisz2023]. Unchecked, the enzyme breaks down the starch framework, which then cannot bind the dough water, and the crumb turns damp and soggy; sourdough acid holds it back, since α-amylase works best at pH 5–5.5 [@science-bread-0022]. The normal falling number of rye types 997 and 1150 is 180–240 s, against 300–360 s for wheat 550 [@science-flour-0032]. Above about 20 % rye in the flour, sourdough or an acidifier also makes the pentosans take up more water and gives an elastic, sliceable crumb [@science-bread-0071]. See [enzymes in dough](/en/learn/enzymes-in-dough) and [sourdough flavour and acidity](/en/learn/sourdough-flavour-and-acidity).

**Handling.** Mix rye dough until even; it needs no kneading [@breadcode-2584]. At 80 % water it is too wet to shape and goes into a greased pan; about 60 % holds a free-standing loaf [@breadcode-0328; @breadcode-0337]. In wheat bread, one creator keeps rye to about 20 % for a good rise [@breadcode-2246]; another source recommends at least 60 % wheat and found no significant loss of volume at 20 % rye [@novita-0316].

## Spelt, einkorn, emmer, khorasan and durum

Spelt, einkorn, emmer and durum carried more protein than bread wheat but more gliadin relative to glutenin, which gives a softer, more extensible and less elastic dough. Grown side by side, their gliadin-to-glutenin ratio averaged 2.5 for bread wheat, 3.3 for spelt, 4.0 for durum, 4.9 for emmer and 6.5 for einkorn [@geisslitz2019]. Wholemeal einkorn, emmer and spelt had 14.5–15.2 % protein on dry matter [@kulathunga2021]. [Gluten and protein](/en/learn/gluten-and-protein) explains why protein and strength part ways.

**Spelt.** Across 90 varieties, white spelt flour absorbed 55.95 % water on the farinograph against 60.0 and 62.9 % for two bread wheats, and its gluten index averaged 59 against 97–100 [@toth2022]. Creators cut the water by about 10 % when spelt replaces half the bread flour [@science-flour-0040] and knead and shape it like wheat [@science-flour-0044]. One creator says the opposite about water, that spelt absorbs a lot, and adds that most spelt sold today is a wheat–spelt mix rather than old spelt [@breadcode-2848].

**Einkorn** is the weakest, with 10.3 mg/g of glutenin against 16.6 for bread wheat [@geisslitz2019]. The dough is slack and sticky: work it with wet hands instead of adding flour [@breadcode-1281], and stir it together without kneading [@breadcode-2761]. One rule of thumb cuts the liquid by roughly 15–20 % and notes a yellow crumb [@science-flour-0041], while a creator's einkorn sourdough uses 65 % water [@breadcode-2700].

**Emmer** is weak too, with 12.8 mg/g of glutenin [@geisslitz2019]. In one baker's side-by-side test, einkorn and emmer doughs built little gluten and were sticky, emmer less so [@breadcode-1925].

**Khorasan and durum** were both weak in one Italian study: khorasan semolato had a W of 57 and a P/L of 1.6 (more strength than extensibility), absorbed 55.8 % and had a falling number of 591 s; durum 'Senatore Cappelli' semolato had a W of 55, absorbed 62.0 % and had 473 s [@nocente2026; @breadcode-0867]. Both falling numbers are well above the 300–360 s that IREKS calls normal for wheat 550, which means little amylase [@science-flour-0032].

Weak, extensible doughs are estimated to tolerate cold proofs of up to about 12 hours [@science-flour-0071], yet one creator keeps shaped einkorn 24 hours in the fridge to build flavour and firm the loaf [@breadcode-2704].

## Gluten-free flours in a wheat dough

- **Buckwheat** is not a gluten cereal but can be contaminated [@science-flour-0046]. It needs more water and mixing than wheat, and about 30 % of the flour is the upper limit in bread [@science-flour-0042]. Its falling number of 918 s points to almost no α-amylase [@wojciechowiczbudzisz2023].
- **Oats** count as a gluten cereal in EU law unless specially produced and below 20 mg/kg of gluten [@science-flour-0046]. Oat bran holds up to 1.6 g of water per gram; replacing 20 % of white flour with bran soaked to that point gave the best bran-enriched loaf in one study [@wu2025].
- **Barley** is a gluten cereal [@science-flour-0046], yet it makes a dense bread: without a scald, a hull-less barley bread was about four times harder than a rye bread, 18 against 4.3 N [@murniece2023]. It also enters wheat bread as malt, about 2 % of the flour in one creator's loaf, for browning and a softer crumb [@breadcode-1853].
- **Maize** has no gluten, so in a Portuguese broa the structure comes from rye [@breadcode-1171], in equal parts [@breadcode-2608] or 70 % maize to 30 % rye [@breadcode-2657].
- **Rice** flour turns up in scalds, where it goes in first because its starch gelatinizes at a higher temperature than wheat's [@novita-0306].
- **Chestnut** flour is dry and starch-poor: 5.5 % water, 46.9 % starch, 12.6 % fibre and 6.1 % protein [@anses2020]. If consistency depends on water per dry matter, the moisture rule turns a flour absorbing 60 % at 14 % moisture into one needing about 76 % at 5.5 %, some 16 points more: arithmetic on an assumption, not a measurement [@science-flour-0056].
