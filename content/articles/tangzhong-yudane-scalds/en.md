A scald is flour or grain mixed with hot liquid so that its starch gelatinizes before the dough is made. Gelatinized starch holds water, so the crumb stays moister and softer and goes stale more slowly (see [storing and freezing bread](/en/learn/storing-and-freezing-bread)) [@breadcode-0876; @novita-0864]. Tangzhong and yudane are the same written characters (湯種) read in Chinese and in Japanese; the English name is water roux [@novita-0109].

## One idea, several names

- **Yudane**: boiling water poured over bread flour, often 1:1 by weight, cooled and refrigerated overnight [@breadcode-0886].
- **Tangzhong**: flour cooked with water or milk into a paste, then cooled and added to the dough [@breadcode-0881]. The English word spread through Yvonne Chen's book on 65 °C bread [@novita-0786; @anon2005]. The classic stovetop version cooks a 1:5 flour-to-water slurry to 65 °C [@novita-0119].
- **Brühstück**: boiling water poured over wholegrain flour or crushed grain [@breadcode-0823]. **Kochstück**: flour or grain heated together with the liquid, stirred to avoid lumps and scorching [@breadcode-0849].
- **Empois** is the French word for the gelatinized starch paste itself.

In Europe scalding was used mainly for rye, for baking quality and shelf life; the Japanese yudane is used on wheat for a sweet, soft, springy crumb [@novita-0110]. Scalding rye also lowers its amylase activity, which otherwise makes the dough soft and sticky [@ask1991].

## What happens in the bowl

Starch gelatinizes when it has excess water (at least about 60 % water by weight) and enough heat. Bread flour is roughly two-thirds starch, so even 1:1 flour to water is enough water; above 60 % water the melting temperature stays near 65 °C [@jang1996; @eliasson1980]. In a 1:2 yudane, granules started to lose their structure at 65 °C and gelatinization was complete at 75 °C [@yamada2015].

The gelatinized starch keeps moisture in the crumb; flour amylases turn part of it into maltose and other sugars, which sweeten the bread and help browning [@novita-0009]. At 40 % yudane, total sugars were about twice the control, crumb moisture rose, and gassing power, gas retention and specific volume fell slightly [@yamauchi2014]. The price is the gluten: protein heated in the scald contributes little strength, so the dough feels firmer but tolerates overmixing less and needs a longer final proof [@novita-0019; @novita-0020].

## How much flour to scald

Express the scald as a share of total flour: "tangzhong 20" means 20 % of all the flour is scalded. A practical guide [@novita-0015]:

| Share of flour | Typical effect |
|---|---|
| 10 % | softer and slower to stale, little loss of height |
| 20 % | standard for milk bread: very soft, slightly chewy, sweeter; visibly lower volume, slightly longer proof |
| 30 % | upper limit: strong anti-staling, clear volume loss (fine for rolls) |
| 40–50 % | sticky dough, heavy volume loss; rarely worth it |

The sources disagree on which level is softest. A Taiwanese master's thesis (Ke Yuwen, 2004) recommends staying at or below 30 %, found 10–20 % softest and saw staling speed up above 30 % [@anon2004; @novita-0005]; in the Japanese study above, the 40 % bread was the softest and staled slowest [@yamauchi2014]. Novita Listyani, who reads both, attributes the gap to how much water each study added [@novita-0114]. A scald does not make bread fluffier: it tends to lower volume [@novita-0001]. The popular 1:5 tangzhong limits the scalded flour to about 5 % of the total, and one paper found its smallest gelatinized-flour dose kept about 96 % of control height, so its effect on volume is small [@carrillonavas2016].

## Ratio and extra water

A 1:1 ratio is technically enough; Novita uses 1.5–2 parts water per part flour because it mixes evenly and holds heat [@novita-0012]. Japanese practice has no fixed ratio: a 1999 patent uses 80–120 g water per 100 g flour [@shibata2001]. A stovetop 1:5 slurry loses an unknown amount of water to evaporation [@novita-0513]; weigh the scald ingredients before cooking and the scald after cooling to measure the loss [@novita-0515]. The rule that a tangzhong dough "needs 75 % hydration" comes from that evaporation and is not needed [@novita-0113].

Count the scald's flour and water in the totals, like a levain [@novita-0138]; [baker's percentages](/en/learn/bakers-percentages) shows how. Then raise the dough water modestly: about 2 points per extra 10 % of flour scalded above 10 % [@novita-0129]; the optimal absorptions she reads from the Japanese data are 68 % (control), 71 % (20 %) and 74 % (40 %), about 1.5 points per 10 % [@novita-0120]. In a 2023 video she read the same table as a rise of under 2 points at 30–40 % [@novita-0514], so treat these as starting points and adjust to your flour. Too little water backfires: in one home test, a 35 % tangzhong bread at about 69 % hydration staled faster than at 73–74 % [@novita-0825]. The [formula calculator](/en/calculators/formula) has a scald stage that carves the flour and water out of the totals and accounts for evaporation.

## 65 °C or 75–80 °C: a method that changed

Novita Listyani's own videos contradict each other here.

- **2023**: stop the scald at 65 °C, because overshooting by about 5 °C inactivates most β-amylase and so reduces maltose; then refrigerate 8–24 h and do not use it straight away [@novita-0119]. Her figures: β-amylase works best at 60–65 °C and is inactivated above 70 °C; α-amylase is inactivated quickly at 80 °C [@novita-0118].
- **2026**: aim for 75–80 °C, made in the microwave (1–2 min) or with boiling water poured into a pre-warmed bowl [@novita-0013]. The reason is a 2017 paper in which gluten heated to 75–80 °C held more water and raised bread volume and softness [@yamada2017]. The scald is used immediately or after 24 h in the fridge; the thesis found 12 h at room temperature or 48 h cold gave harder bread [@novita-0004]. An industrial yudane process ages the scald cold for 15–72 h [@pascond].

The two are not reconciled: by her 2023 enzyme figures, a 75–80 °C scald should make less maltose. No side-by-side test in the sources settles it, and both positions rest on her reading of the papers, so treat either as a starting point.

## Making it

Pour the hot water into a pre-warmed bowl first, add the flour and mix within about a minute; a watery or loose scald usually means it cooled too fast [@novita-0804; @novita-0803]. Rice flour gelatinizes at up to about 80 °C, so it goes in first [@novita-0498; @novita-0244]. Break a cold scald up in part of the recipe water before mixing, or it leaves lumps [@novita-0193]. A scalded dough looks stronger than it is: still check the windowpane (see [mixing, rests and folds](/en/learn/mixing-and-gluten)) [@novita-0684].
