Flour, water and salt are the essential ingredients of a wheat sourdough [@breadcode-0380]. Salt seasons the bread, but it also tightens the gluten and slows the yeast [@bakerpedianda].

## How much

Salt is given in baker's percentage, as a share of the flour weight (see [baker's percentages](/en/learn/bakers-percentages)); the [formula calculator](/en/calculators/formula) turns it into grams.

| Source | Salt, % of flour | Context |
|---|---|---|
| BAKERpedia | 1.8–2.0 | bread; 0.75–1.25 in cookies and cakes, for flavour only [@bakerpedianda] |
| *The Sourdough Framework* | about 2 | wheat sourdough [@breadcode-0386] |
| The Bread Code | 1–3, uses 2 | salt as a flavour enhancer [@breadcode-1123] |
| Carcea et al. | 1.5 is enough | the authors' conclusion for dough quality; taste not assessed [@carceam2020] |

Bases differ: for sourdough, an older Bread Code guide takes the 2 % on the whole dough mass, sourdough included, rather than on the flour [@breadcode-1124].

## Gluten and dough handling

In 176 Italian soft-wheat flours, 1.5 % salt lowered the farinograph water absorption by a few points in 95 % of samples, raised stability in 70 % and raised the alveograph strength (W) by up to 86 %. With that salt, medium bread flours tolerated longer mixing and fermentation; strong flours did well without salt and became too tenacious at 3 % [@carceam2020].

Novita Listyani explains that at dough pH gluten proteins carry a net positive charge, which salt shields so that the network tightens [@novita-0249]. Citing Galal and Johnson (1977), she adds that in sourdough the acids add charge, pushing the strands apart (more extensible but weaker and stickier), and that salt shields it, so salt matters more for structure in sourdough [@novita-0054; @am1977]. In her single, subjective hand-mixed comparison, the unsalted dough was stickier and weaker [@novita-0734].

## Fermentation

Salt slows the yeast by osmosis [@bakerpedianda]; according to Novita Listyani, at bread levels, about 2 %, it kills neither yeast nor a starter [@novita-0052; @yeh2009]. Over a 3-hour test, total gas from one flour fell from 1518 mL without salt to 1487 mL at 1.5 % and 1272 mL at 3 %, while the share of gas retained rose [@carceam2020]. The fermentation model built on these data runs about 4 % faster at 1.5 % salt and 12 % slower at 3 % than at 2 % [@science-bread-0051]. For sourdough, a model tuned on test doughs has 2 % salt, against none, lengthening the time to double by about 15 % and the bulk by 21 % [@science-ferm-0027]; in broth, a sourdough lactobacillus was inhibited at 4 % salt while the yeast tolerated 8 % (weight per volume of broth, not baker's percentage) [@ganzlemg1998].

Novita's experience points to a bigger effect. In a side-by-side demonstration, the dough with enough salt took roughly twice as long to proof [@novita-0733], and in bagels she finds that too little salt lets the dough overproof quickly while too much leaves it dense [@novita-0332]. Her demonstration is a single comparison; the measured and modelled effects above are much smaller. The [fermentation calculator](/en/calculators/fermentation) applies that model's factor to yeast doughs (salt capped at 3.5 %) and, for sourdough, a straight line from the Bread Code's table: 9 % of the rate per percentage point of salt, 1.00 at 2 %, capped at 3 % [@science-ferm-0027].

## Flavour, crust and keeping

Novita Listyani quotes an unnamed study in which salt-free bread had larger air cells, tasted more sour, acidic and yeasty, and was significantly harder after about 5 days, though not between 2 and 50 hours [@novita-0735]. In a controlled study as she reports it, cutting the salt of a lean bread from 1.2 % to 0.6, 0.3 or 0 % shortened its shelf life by about 2 days, from 5–6 to 3–4 days; with an antifungal sourdough, the salt level no longer made a significant difference [@novita-0738; @novita-0739; @belz2012]. [Storing and freezing bread](/en/learn/storing-and-freezing-bread) covers the rest.

## Cutting salt: what studies found

These studies reach this page through Novita Listyani's summaries.

- A one-quarter reduction went unnoticed by consumers when it was made in 5 % steps over 6 weeks [@novita-0742; @girgis2003].
- In 116 people eating buffet breakfasts over 4 weeks, cutting the salt of brown bread by about 50 % (52 % in her account) did not raise the sodium they took from fillings; at 67 % they ate less bread, unless potassium chloride and yeast extract made up the difference [@novita-0743; @bolhuis2011].
- Layering doughs of different saltiness cut salt by 28 % with no loss of perceived saltiness; by her arithmetic, that takes a 1.8 % bread below 1.3 %, though the effect was weaker with a trained panel [@novita-0746; @noort2010].
- A longer proof makes bread taste saltier, because coarser, larger pores release sodium faster in the mouth; pores that grow too large give a lumpy crumb that releases it more slowly [@novita-0744; @pflaum2013].

## When to add it

For Novita, Calvel's autolyse left salt out because it served no purpose there, not to protect the yeast; what holding salt back really does is let the flour's proteases soften the gluten a little before salt brakes them [@novita-0053]. The Bread Code's author reports little difference in informal tests: in the Framework, none between salt added at the start or at the end of the autolyse, and he calls late salting a myth [@breadcode-0438]; in a single uncontrolled batch, salt in the autolyse did not noticeably slow gluten development [@breadcode-2895]. Late salt has a cost: working salt and starter into a rested dough amounts to kneading it again [@breadcode-0449]. Rests are covered in [mixing, rests and folds](/en/learn/mixing-and-gluten), proteases in [enzymes in dough](/en/learn/enzymes-in-dough).

## Sea salt and coarse salt

None of the sources used here compares sea salt with refined salt inside the dough. On the tongue, crystal shape matters: as Novita Listyani reports a study, compact cubic rock-salt crystals dissolved about 3.6 times more slowly in artificial saliva than agglomerated sea or flake salts, giving a lower peak of saltiness [@novita-0572; @quilaqueo2015]. This article infers that it concerns only crystals that stay whole, as on a topping. In one test of her own, an unrefined sea salt sprinkled on top turned grey or black in the oven, while pretzel salt stayed white [@novita-0573].
