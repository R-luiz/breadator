A flour's type number tells you how much mineral matter, or ash, is left when the flour is burnt. Ash comes mostly from the outer layers of the grain, so a higher number means a flour closer to wholemeal and usually heartier in taste [@breadcode-0251; @breadcode-0252]. It tells you how dark the flour is, but nothing about baking strength [@breadcode-1400].

## Four national systems

**France.** Since 1963 wheat flour types have been defined by ash on dry matter: T45 below 0.50 %, T55 0.50–0.60 %, T65 0.62–0.75 %, T80 0.75–0.90 %, T110 1.00–1.20 % and T150 above 1.40 % [@science-flour-0001]. T130 is a trade name for a wholemeal flour of about 1.2–1.4 %, not a legal type [@science-flour-0002].

**Germany.** Under DIN 10355 the number is the mean ash in milligrams per 100 g of dry matter: 405 up to 0.50 %, 550 0.51–0.63 %, 812 0.64–0.90 %, 1050 0.91–1.20 % and 1600 1.21–1.80 % [@science-flour-0011]. Wholemeal flour keeps the germ and carries no type number [@science-flour-0012].

**Italy.** The law sets a maximum ash and a minimum protein, both on dry matter: 00 at most 0.55 % ash and at least 9.00 % protein, 0 at most 0.65 % and 11.00 %, 1 0.80 % and 12.00 %, 2 0.95 % and 12.00 %, integrale 1.30–1.70 % ash and 12.00 % protein [@science-flour-0014].

**United States.** Flour is not typed by ash. Its ash, moisture-free, may not exceed one twentieth of its protein plus 0.35, so a 12 % protein flour may carry up to 0.95 % [@science-flour-0016]. All-purpose and bread flour are commercial protein classes, not legal standards: about 10.3–11.7 % for all-purpose and 11.5–13.5 % for bread flour [@usdepartmentofagriculture2018; @science-flour-0021; @science-flour-0022]; one large miller lists 14 % for its high-gluten flour [@science-flour-0022].

## Rough equivalents

*The Sourdough Framework* lines up the labels of the four countries [@science-flour-0045]. The table follows its rows, adds T65, which it leaves out, and gives the ash limits on dry matter and the French use of each type [@science-flour-0100].

| Usual role | France | Germany | Italy | United States |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cakes, pastry | T45 (< 0.50 %) | 405 (≤ 0.50 %) | 00 (≤ 0.55 %) | cake flour |
| Everyday white | T55 (0.50–0.60 %) | 550 (0.51–0.63 %) | 0 (≤ 0.65 %) | all-purpose |
| Most French breads | T65 (0.62–0.75 %) | low end of 812 | 0 or 1 | — |
| Light brown | T80 (0.75–0.90 %) | 812 (0.64–0.90 %) | 1 (≤ 0.80 %) | — |
| Semi-wholemeal | T110 (1.00–1.20 %) | 1050 (0.91–1.20 %) | 2 (≤ 0.95 %) | — |
| Wholemeal | T150 (> 1.40 %) | Vollkorn (no number) | integrale (1.30–1.70 %) | whole wheat |

These are approximations: by ash, Italian 0 spans T55 and the lower T65, German 812 spans T65 and T80, and the US classes have no ash equivalent [@science-flour-0045]. "Bread flour" fits no single row: the same book places it at T45 or T55, 405 or 550, 00 or 0 [@breadcode-0253], because it is defined by protein, which the type does not record [@science-flour-0021; @breadcode-1400].

## What varies from one bag to the next

**Protein.** Pooled commercial French flours had about the same protein in every wheat type, 11.4–11.9 % (N × 6.25, as sold), while fibre rose from 3.9 % in T55 to 12.5 % in T150 [@bourre2008]; the French food composition table gives only 9.9 % for its T55 [@science-flour-0008]. The type is no guide to protein, and the figure depends on the basis it is stated on: see [gluten and protein](/en/learn/gluten-and-protein).

**Strength.** The alveograph W is the energy needed to blow a sheet of dough into a bubble until it bursts; a high W suits long fermentation, a low W cakes and pastry [@breadcode-0883; @breadcode-0884]. Modern French T55 for baguettes has a medium W of 160–250 [@calvel2001].

**Enzymes.** The falling number measures α-amylase backwards: the fewer seconds, the more enzyme. For German type 550, 300–360 s is normal; at 240–300 s the dough develops faster, is slightly damp and colours more, at 360–420 s the proof is weak, the crust pale and the volume small [@science-flour-0032]. [Enzymes in dough](/en/learn/enzymes-in-dough) goes further.

## Water and fridge time by class

Process counts as much as flour: the same T65 takes 62 % water in a straight-dough baguette and 68 % in a long-fermented tradition dough [@bourre2008]. No study has measured how long each flour tolerates the fridge; the last column is an estimate built on the rule that stronger flour tolerates longer fermentation [@science-flour-0071; @science-flour-0043]. The water column is the calculator's starting value and range, an estimate anchored on the references below.

| Flour | Starting water in bread (range) | Cold proof at 4–6 °C (estimate) |
|---|---|---|
| Pastry (T45, 405) | 58–60 % (55–65) | up to 12 h |
| Medium white (T55, T65, 550, 0, all-purpose) | 62–66 % (56–72) | 12–24 h, 48 h with care [@breadcode-0611] |
| Strong white (US bread and high-gluten) | 66–68 % (56–78) | up to 48–72 h |
| High extraction (T80, T110, 812, 1050, 1, 2) | 66–68 % (60–74) | 12–24 h |
| Wholemeal (T150, Vollkorn, integrale, whole wheat) | 72–78 % (66–85) | 12–24 h |
| Italian 00, weak to very strong by W [@science-flour-0043] | 62 % (55–75) | up to 48 h |

French reference recipes use 62–68 % for T55 and T65, 66 % for T80 and T110, 70 % for T150 [@bourre2008]; German references 60–62 % for wheat bread [@gmbhnd] and 76–80 % for wholemeal doughs [@loderbauer2008]; hard spring-wheat flours took 68–71 % in a laboratory baking test [@pradhan2026]. The pastry row is an estimate from the lower absorption of low-ash flours [@science-flour-0070]. See [retarding dough in the fridge](/en/learn/cold-retard).

## Adapting a recipe from another country

- **Match protein, not the type number.** Protein is the best quality indicator on the pack [@breadcode-0263]. A recipe for US bread flour expects 11.5–13.5 % protein at 14 % moisture [@science-flour-0021]: look for that, on the same basis, whatever the type.
- **Keep the recipe's water, then correct it.** Shift it by about 1 point per point of protein [@tipples1978] and by 5.5–8 points between white and wholemeal [@pradhan2026]. One creator goes from 70 % to 65 % water when a 12 % protein flour replaces his 15 % one [@breadcode-1912]. Judge the consistency of the dough rather than trusting the recipe's water [@breadcode-2653].
- **Shorten the clock for a weaker flour.** Fermentations longer than about 8 hours call for a stronger flour [@breadcode-0039]; in one trial a weak, cheap flour collapsed after about 10 hours [@breadcode-2355]. Shorten bulk and the cold proof, or raise the protein with vital gluten [@science-flour-0060].
