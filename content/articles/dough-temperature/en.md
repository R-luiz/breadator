Warmer dough ferments faster and colder dough ferments slower [@breadcode-0425]. Dough temperature is also the one fermentation variable that is decided in the few minutes of mixing, by the water, the flour, the room and the heat of kneading. Bakers therefore choose a desired dough temperature (DDT) and work back to the water temperature. For wheat breads raised with yeast, 24–26 °C is the recommended target [@science-ferm-0075].

## Why one degree matters

For commercial yeast, a pizza-dough fermentation chart tuned on bakers' feedback implies that 10 °C more divides the time by about 3.9 near 20 °C and 3.4 near 25 °C, which is 13–15 % faster per degree [@science-ferm-0015]. Tables and recipes that list several temperatures are flatter, about 2.1 per 10 °C or 8 % per degree [@science-ferm-0016], and a French trade rule equates 1 °C on the dough with 10 % of the yeast weight [@science-ferm-0017]. For sourdough, the published growth curves of a sourdough yeast and its lactobacilli, blended with equal weight, give 0.58 of the 25 °C rate at 20 °C and 1.17 at 28 °C [@science-ferm-0040]. Whichever figure is right for your culture, a dough that comes out 3 °C off target multiplies or divides the fermentation time by about 1.25 to 1.5.

Temperature also acts on the dough itself: it turns sticky and less stable above about 27 °C, and mixing at 16 °C raised elasticity by about 21 % in one study [@basaran2003; @rosell2009].

## The French base temperature

French bakers add three temperatures to a fixed sum, the *température de base*: water = base − (room + flour). Indicative bases are 68–72 for slow kneading, 60–64 for improved kneading and 52–54 for intensive kneading, on an oblique-axis mixer, with the lower figure in summer [@science-ferm-0070]. With a base of 64, a room at 22 °C and flour at 20 °C, the water goes in at 22 °C. To move the mixed dough by 1 °C, change the base by about 3 [@science-ferm-0071].

## Three and four factors

The American version states the target openly: water = 3 × DDT − room − flour − friction factor. With a preferment, it becomes 4 × DDT − room − flour − preferment − friction factor [@science-ferm-0072]. Novita Listyani uses the three-factor form in a hot, humid climate, where the answer is iced water [@novita-0773]. It is the same arithmetic as the French rule: base = 3 × DDT − friction factor. For a 24 °C dough, the French bases correspond to friction factors of 0–4 °C for slow kneading, 8–12 °C for improved and 18–20 °C for intensive kneading [@science-ferm-0074].

## Friction

| Mixing | Friction factor (3-factor formula) |
|---|---|
| Gentle folds by hand | 0–2 °C |
| Vigorous hand kneading, 8 min | 3–4 °C |
| Planetary stand mixer, 3 min slow + 4 min on speed 2 | 12–13 °C |
| "Most mixers", as quoted from Jeffrey Hamelman | 13–16 °C |

These are one test baker's measurements and one quotation [@science-ferm-0073]. The friction factor is a number in the units of the formula, not the warming of the dough: with three factors the dough itself warms by about a third of it, roughly 4 °C for the stand mixer above. The French bases imply a real rise of 0–1.3 °C for slow, 2.7–4 °C for improved and 6–6.7 °C for intensive kneading [@science-ferm-0074]. Fast mixing heats more [@breadcode-1939] (see [mixing, rests and folds](/en/learn/mixing-and-gluten)), and a rich dough that must be mixed long is held down with cold butter added in portions [@novita-0462].

No table replaces one measurement on your own mixer. Take the dough's temperature after mixing and solve the formula for the friction factor: 3 × measured dough temperature − room − flour − water. If the next dough comes out 1 °C too warm, raise the factor by 3, the same "3 per degree" as the French rule [@science-ferm-0071].

## The heat-balance view

The formulas above give water, flour and room the same weight. In fact a gram of water holds far more heat than a gram of flour: handbook specific heats are 4.19 kJ/kg·K for water, 1.59 for flour, 3.77–3.93 for whole milk, 3.18 for whole eggs and 2.72 for butter [@science-ferm-0076; @science-ferm-0077]. A heat balance weights each ingredient by its mass times its specific heat, then adds the real warming from mixing. The same rule gives about 2.8 J/g·K for a dough at 70 % hydration, 3.0 for a levain at 100 % hydration and 2.6 for a stiff levain [@science-ferm-0078].

A worked case: 1,000 g of flour at 20 °C and 700 g of water, flour taken at 1.8 J/g·K. The flour holds 1,800 J per degree and the water 2,926. To land at 25 °C with 4 °C of mixing heat, the blend must be at 21 °C, so the water at 21.6 °C; the three-factor formula with a friction factor of 12 and a 20 °C room says 23 °C. The answers differ because the simple rule gives water, flour and room the same weight and folds everything else into the friction factor; the heat balance weighs masses and specific heats, and it handles what the simple rules ignore: milk, eggs, butter, a levain or a scald at their own temperatures. A tangzhong and a poolish taken cold from the fridge are a deliberate way to bring the dough temperature down in a warm room [@novita-0493].

## Ice

In a warm kitchen, iced water keeps the dough from over-fermenting [@novita-0847]. Ice cools mostly by melting: a gram takes 334 J to melt (the latent heat of fusion of water, the value the calculator uses), as much heat as cooling a gram of water by 80 °C, since water holds 4.19 J/g·K [@science-ferm-0077]. To get the equivalent of 5 °C water from tap water at 18 °C, about 13 % of the water weight goes in as ice and the rest as tap water. When the calculation asks for water below 0 °C, ice in the water cannot reach the target alone: cool the flour or shorten the mix.

Cold mixing has a second benefit. Doughs mixed at lower temperatures gave better texture and volume in a 2003 study, and cooling with CO2 snow during kneading also improved volume [@basaran2003; @cappelli2020a]. In a noodle-dough study at the same final dough temperature, cold water with warmer flour gave the strongest gluten network after about 9 minutes of mixing, although hot water with cold flour gave a stronger dough after 3 minutes [@li2025].

## The simple case

A dough mixed by hand and built with folds has almost no friction, so water at room temperature gives a dough at room temperature. *The Sourdough Framework* advises exactly that, with 30 °C water when the room is at 30 °C [@breadcode-1098]. In a cool kitchen, warmer water speeds things up; about 30 °C is plenty [@breadcode-2595].
