Mixing hydrates the flour, spreads the ingredients evenly and starts the gluten network. It does not have to finish the job: gluten keeps developing during rests, folds and shaping, so a dough that is only partly developed after mixing can be fully developed by the time it is shaped [@novita-0046]. Think of mixing, rests and folds as one budget of work, spent in whichever way suits the dough.

## Autolyse, fermentolyse or a simple rest

Raymond Calvel described autolyse in 1974: flour and water mixed slowly for a few minutes, then rested 15–30 minutes before the yeast and salt go in. It shortened the intensive mixing that was bleaching and oxidising French dough, and gave a more extensible dough, easier shaping and better volume [@calvel2001]. In such a short rest little amylase or protease work happens, so claims of big flavour gains from autolyse are weak [@novita-0105]. *The Sourdough Framework* describes the rest more broadly, as a head start for flour enzymes that shortens bulk and improves browning [@breadcode-0805]; home sourdough recipes often stretch it to 1–2 hours or overnight [@breadcode-1790]. Longer is not always better: in one study, resting beyond about 60 minutes reduced the glutenin macropolymer that gives gluten its strength [@feng2021].

**Fermentolyse** skips the separate step: the starter goes in from the start, at about 10 % of the flour in warm weather and up to 20 % in cold weather [@breadcode-0837; @breadcode-1697]; [levain and inoculation](/en/learn/levain-and-inoculation) covers the dose. With a modest inoculation the enzymes do the same work during bulk, so one fermentation of about 7 hours replaces a 2-hour autolyse plus 5 hours of bulk, with one mix instead of two [@breadcode-0450]. For yeasted doughs the same author lowers the yeast rather than autolysing [@breadcode-0465].

The benefit comes from resting, wherever the rest falls: at the start, between folds or during bulk. If you skip autolyse, add a rest or one more set of folds [@novita-0108]. Holding the salt back lets flour proteases soften the gluten a little; adding it tightens the dough [@novita-0053], but in one baker's tests salt in the autolyse did not noticeably slow gluten development [@breadcode-2895]. In a side-by-side bake, about 5 minutes of hands-on work and about an hour of autolyse, kneading, lamination and folds gave only a marginal difference [@breadcode-2240].

## Bassinage

Bassinage holds back part of the water: the dough is mixed at a lower hydration until the gluten forms, then the rest is worked in [@breadcode-0818]. In refined-flour dough, adding the water in two equal parts, or in three parts of 40, 40 and 20 %, gave the best elasticity and tensile strength [@yang2019]; in wholewheat dough, gradual addition improved extensibility whatever the total water [@parenti2021]. The reason: in very wet dough glutenin moves freely, so a network forms fast but tears easily when mixed further; a stiffer start gives more friction and alignment, and the developed network then takes up the extra water instead of turning sticky [@novita-0099]. Wetter doughs are also damaged more by overmixing [@jia2022].

## Mixing order

In a stand mixer, put the liquid in first so no dry flour sits at the bottom of the bowl [@breadcode-1678]. Break a tangzhong (see [scalds](/en/learn/tangzhong-yudane-scalds)) up in part of the recipe's water before it goes into the mixer, so it does not form lumps [@novita-0017]. Fat goes in last: it interferes with gluten development, so mix 3–4 minutes first, add soft butter, then mix about 5–7 minutes more [@novita-0339; @novita-0356]; [enriched doughs](/en/learn/enriched-doughs) goes further. The dough seems to fall apart when the butter goes in, then comes back together and turns smooth [@novita-0768]. Egg yolk fat also lengthens mixing [@novita-0801]. Soak dried fruit before adding it, or it pulls water out of the dough [@novita-0195].

## The windowpane, and when to test it

Stretching a piece of dough into a thin film is a home version of the alveograph: both stretch dough in two directions at once, the same way gas cells do during proof and oven spring [@dufour2024]. Compare like with like: an 80 % hydration dough passes with a more fragile film than a 60 % one [@novita-0048]. A no-knead or folded dough fails right after mixing; test it after the last set of folds [@novita-0049]. If a dough still fails after much more mixing than expected, stop and let it rest rather than mixing on [@novita-0047]: overmixed dough turns slack and sticky [@novita-0327].

## Stretch-and-folds and coil folds

Above roughly 70–80 % hydration, kneading by hand becomes impractical and folds take over [@novita-0280]. A common pattern is 4 sets, up to 8 for a weak dough, spaced 20 minutes to an hour apart [@novita-0281]. The coil fold (with wet hands, lift the dough from each side and let it fold over itself) is the gentlest version and suits the whole of bulk [@breadcode-0828]. Fold when the dough has visibly spread, not by the clock [@breadcode-2564]: each fold evens out the large bubbles, so fewer folds favour a more open crumb [@breadcode-2566]. Slap-and-fold works only for wet doughs [@novita-0231]; it is messy and adds friction, and regular folds build similar strength [@breadcode-1940].

## Dough temperature during mixing

Mixing warms the dough by friction, and a few degrees change how fast it ferments [@breadcode-1939]. One test baker measured about 12–13 °C of friction factor for a stand mixer, 3–4 °C for 8 minutes of vigorous hand kneading and 0–2 °C for gentle folds [@science-ferm-0073]. Cool mixing also helps the gluten itself: dough turns sticky and less stable above about 27 °C, and mixing at 16 °C raised elasticity by about 21 % in one study [@basaran2003; @rosell2009]. A cold dough needs longer mixing, so judge by feel and the windowpane rather than the timer [@cappelli2020b]; cold preferments add about 2–3 minutes [@novita-0401]. In a noodle-dough study at the same final temperature, cold water with warmer flour gave the strongest network after about 9 minutes of mixing, though hot water with cold flour was stronger after 3 [@li2025]. [Dough temperature](/en/learn/dough-temperature) explains the friction factor, and the [temperature calculator](/en/calculators/temperature) gives the water (or ice) temperature for your target dough temperature, flour and mixing heat.
