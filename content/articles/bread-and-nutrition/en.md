Health benefits are often attributed to sourdough bread. Two reviews, as summarised by Novita Listyani, found no clinically established benefit over yeast bread [@novita-0145; @damico2023; @ribet2023]. What follows describes studies, not advice.

## Blood glucose: sourdough against yeast bread

A meta-analysis of 18 randomised trials in adults found a smaller rise in serum glucose after sourdough bread than after industrial bread or a glucose drink, pooled: mean difference −0.29 at 60 minutes (95 % CI −0.46 to −0.12) and −0.21 at 120 minutes (−0.32 to −0.09), unit not given in the abstract. Certainty was low to very low, fasting insulin did not change, and the effect was clearest for whole-wheat sourdough [@rolim2022]. Assessing a high-fibre sourdough rye bread, EFSA found lower responses than with glucose but judged the effect not specific, since almost any carbohydrate food does the same; it repeated this in 2025 [@efsapanelondieteticproducts2014; @turck2025].

A systematic review of 20 studies (369 people) found, in healthy subjects, a significantly lower response in 3 of 8 same-recipe comparisons of sourdough and yeast bread and in 2 of 7 comparisons of whole-grain sourdough with white yeast bread [@ribet2023]. A 2026 review of 27 studies in healthy adults judged the evidence of a benefit on glycaemia and insulin "neither sufficient nor convincing" and too varied to pool [@starowicz2026].

## Freezing, toasting and resistant starch

Cooled gelatinised starch retrogrades into type-3 resistant starch, which escapes digestion in the small intestine; a review shows it in cooked potato (0.64 to 1.30 g/100 g after 24 hours at 4 °C), not bread [@murillo2022]. For bread the sources hold one small study, which measured glucose response, not resistant starch: in 10 healthy adults the two-hour incremental glucose area after homemade white bread was 259 mmol·min/L fresh, 179 frozen and defrosted, 193 toasted and 157 frozen then toasted [@burton2007]. No source read measured resistant starch in cooled, frozen or toasted bread, so no amount or glycaemic index can be given [@science-bread-0118]; see [storing and freezing bread](/en/learn/storing-and-freezing-bread).

## Fructans and FODMAPs

According to Novita Listyani, fructans and other FODMAPs trigger symptoms in some people with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) [@novita-0153]. In refined-wheat yeast bread, a final proof of 80 instead of 60 minutes lowered fructans from 338 to 266 mg/100 g dry matter; sourdough lowered fructans but raised mannitol, so total FODMAPs did not fall; refined-wheat breads met low-FODMAP criteria, rye and whole-meal wheat breads did not, under every process tested [@schmidt2020]. In rye, 180 instead of 90 minutes of yeast [fermentation](/en/learn/fermentation-time-and-temperature) did not lower fructans; a *Lactobacillus plantarum* sourdough did [@pejcz2020]. A review notes that yeast invertase degrades mainly short fructans and that low-FODMAP bread needs organisms with fructanase; in an IBS crossover trial a rye bread made that way, with a third of the FODMAPs, caused less flatulence, pain and rumbling than regular rye bread [@loponen2018], while in Novita Listyani's summary of IBS trials the severity score did not improve [@novita-0153].

## Phytate and minerals

The flour's own phytase degrades phytate best between pH 4.3 and 5.5 [@ribet2026]. In vitro, bringing a whole-wheat dough to about pH 5.5, with sourdough or lactic acid alone, broke down 70 % of its phytate against 40 % without acidification [@leenhardt2004]; in whole-wheat bread, sourdough removed 62 % and yeast 38 % [@lopez2001]. Better mineral status is not established: a 2026 review (8 human studies) found the effect on iron inconclusive, with single meals of low-phytate bread improving absorption but 12 weeks of low-phytate sourdough rye bread not improving iron status (ferritin 32 to 27 µg/L) [@nikolaou2026].

## Fibre and β-glucan: the EU claims

Regulation (EU) 432/2012 authorises two claims for β-glucans from oats and barley. For normal blood cholesterol: "The claim may be used only for food which contains at least 1 g of beta-glucans from oats, oat bran, barley, barley bran, or from mixtures of these sources per quantified portion", with the consumer informed that the effect comes with a daily intake of 3 g. For a smaller blood-glucose rise after a meal: "The claim may be used only for food which contains at least 4 g of beta-glucans from oats or barley for each 30 g of available carbohydrates in a quantified portion as part of the meal", with the consumer informed that the effect comes from consuming them as part of the meal [@commission2012].

As an extrapolation, not a measurement: with 44–54 g of available carbohydrate per 100 g of bread, the post-meal claim would need about 5.9–7.3 g of β-glucan per 100 g of bread, and the cholesterol claim 2 g per 100 g for a 50 g portion; flour or bran weight is not β-glucan weight [@science-bread-0122].

## Coeliac disease

Sourdough fermentation does not make bread from wheat (spelt, einkorn, emmer and khorasan included), rye or barley safe for people with coeliac disease [@science-bread-0137]. Beyond Celiac says it lowers the gluten of regular sourdough bread but not to 20 ppm or less, the US gluten-free limit, and that gut damage can occur without symptoms [@celiacnd]; the EU "gluten-free" limit is also 20 mg/kg [@commission2014].

The breads tolerated in trials were experimental, made with selected lactobacilli and fungal proteases (or mostly gluten-free flours), not home or bakery sourdough; nothing here shows that a home loaf can reach their gluten levels [@science-bread-0137]. In a 60-day trial in coeliac patients eating 200 g of baked goods a day, flour hydrolysed to 2,480 ppm gluten caused villous atrophy, without complaints, in both patients who ate it; only flour hydrolysed to 8 ppm left antibodies and gut lining unchanged, in 5 patients [@greco2011]. In one challenge, 13 of 17 patients showed altered intestinal permeability after yeast bread but not after a 30 %-wheat bread with oat, millet and buckwheat, fermented 24 hours with selected lactobacilli [@dicagno2004].

## Salt

As reported by Novita Listyani, in a 4-week trial with 116 people, cutting the salt of brown bread by about 50 % (52 % in her account) did not raise the sodium they took from sandwich fillings [@novita-0743; @bolhuis2011]; see [salt in bread](/en/learn/salt-in-bread).
