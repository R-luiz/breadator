Fresh and dry yeast are the same organism sold with different amounts of water [@breadcode-2269]. With a pure yeast, fermentation is reproducible as long as temperature, flour and strain stay the same [@breadcode-0006]. What the baker chooses is the form, and above all the dose.

## The forms

| Form | Dry matter | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh (compressed, cake) | 31–34 % | crumbled into the dough |
| Active dry | about 93 % | rehydrated first |
| Instant dry | about 95 % | mixed straight into the flour |

The dry-matter figures come from a French professional training site [@science-ferm-0024]. Active dry yeast recovers best when rehydrated near 40 °C; in cold water its cells leak their contents, including glutathione, which slackens the dough [@science-ferm-0025]. Instant yeast should not be soaked first, for the same reason, and goes onto the flour instead of into the liquids [@novita-0621; @novita-0676]. When instant replaces fresh yeast, a manufacturer advises adding 2 % of water for every 1 % of instant yeast [@science-ferm-0022].

Osmotolerant yeast is a different strain, selected for sweet doughs, which [enriched doughs](/en/learn/enriched-doughs) often are; glycerol and trehalose help it withstand sugar [@novita-0625].

## Conversions, and why they differ

- A yeast manufacturer's professional bulletin: instant yeast holds about 3.3 times the solids of fresh yeast, the rule of thumb is 3 parts fresh for 1 part instant, and the best ratio depends on the fresh yeast, the recipe and the way it is added [@science-ferm-0020]. Another issue of the same bulletin says 3 to 4 parts [@science-ferm-0021].
- Two consumer brands: a 17 g cake of fresh yeast equals one 7 g packet of dry yeast of either type, a ratio of 2.4 to 1 [@science-ferm-0023].
- A pizza-dough fermentation chart: 1 % fresh = 0.42 % active dry = 0.32 % instant [@science-ferm-0002].
- Bread creators: fresh at three times the weight of dry [@breadcode-1492; @novita-0808].

So one gram of fresh yeast is worth 0.25 to 0.42 g of instant yeast, with one third as the usual figure, and active dry yeast is worth 0.75 to 1 times its weight in instant, usually 0.75. The dry-matter ratio supports 3 to 1 for instant yeast; the consumer figure fits active dry better. The spread matters less than it seems, because time does not follow the dose in proportion: with the dose law below, a dose 25 % too high shortens fermentation by about 15 % [@science-ferm-0006]. The [yeast converter](/en/calculators/yeast) gives the range with each conversion.

## Dose against time

A French direct dough uses about 1.5–2 % fresh yeast on flour, and the doses quoted run from 10 to 24 g per kg [@science-ferm-0013]. At the other end, one creator uses about 0.25 % dry yeast for a dough that doubles overnight [@breadcode-2716] and 0.1 % for a slow ciabatta [@breadcode-1491], and *The Sourdough Framework*'s default pizza dough holds 0.015 % [@breadcode-0414].

A chart of fermentation times fitted across 0.003–0.32 % instant yeast gives hours to double = 0.91 × dose^−0.72 at 25 °C, with the dose in percent of instant yeast [@science-ferm-0006]. For a lean dough that means roughly 1 hour at 1 % (beyond the chart's range), 5 hours at 0.1 % and 8 hours at 0.05 %; at 18 °C, about 2.5, 12 and 20 hours. Ten times less yeast is about five times more time, not ten. [Fermentation time and temperature](/en/learn/fermentation-time-and-temperature) gives the full table and its limits.

The dose also compensates for temperature (see [dough temperature](/en/learn/dough-temperature)): a French trade rule counts 1 °C on the dough as 10 % of the yeast weight [@science-ferm-0017].

## Sugar and salt

A little sugar feeds the yeast; more of it holds the yeast back by osmotic pressure. One manufacturer puts the turning point at about 5 % of the flour [@science-ferm-0030]. A figure from the same maker shows that it depends on the strain: a lean-dough yeast at about 100 % relative activity without sugar, 80 % at 10 % sugar, 60 % at 20 % and 40 % at 30 %, against a sugar-tolerant yeast at 57, 78, 100 and 125 % [@science-ferm-0029]. The two lines cross near 10 % sugar, the level above which the same maker advises raising the dose [@science-ferm-0022]. The osmotic load is measurable: a dough with 18 % sugar and 45 % water had a water activity of 0.95, against 0.97 with 6 % sugar and 52 % water [@aslankoohie2015]. Sources differ on where to switch strains: Novita Listyani keeps regular instant yeast at 16 % sugar and accepts a somewhat longer fermentation [@novita-0820].

Salt at bread levels, about 2 %, does not kill yeast [@yeh2009]. It slows it slightly: over 3 hours, total gas was 0.96–0.98 of the unsalted value at 1.5 % salt and 0.78–0.84 at 3 % [@carceam2020].

## Tiny dose, long ferment: the debate

**For a small dose.** The Framework holds that very little yeast with at least 8 hours of fermentation gives a much more open, lighter crumb [@breadcode-0413], and its author's videos add that too much yeast ferments the dough faster than the flour can be broken down [@breadcode-1493]. Raymond Calvel's view was that more yeast shortens bulk at the price of a yeasty taste, less flavour and faster staling [@calvel2001]. Novita Listyani agrees for a long room-temperature schedule: the aim is aroma compounds, not gas, so excess yeast is pointless [@novita-0577].

**Against a minuscule dose in the cold.** The same creator argues that long fridge ferments with almost no yeast are a bad idea: the yeast cannot consume its substrate in time, which leaves unfermented dough to other microbes, and too little yeast takes up too little oxygen, so fats oxidise into off-flavours [@novita-0073]. In the study she relies on, several pleasant aroma compounds increased with more yeast, while most lipid-oxidation products did not depend on the dose [@birch2013b]. A food-safety study adds that *Salmonella* and *Staphylococcus aureus* can grow in a no-knead dough during a prolonged room-temperature fermentation [@pao2011]. Too much is not better either: excess yeast activity over a long ferment can release glutathione, which destabilises the dough [@novita-0299].

The two sides describe different schedules. A small dose at room temperature still ferments the dough fully, only slowly. The same dose in the fridge may barely ferment it. A middle path is to split the yeast: a little in a long preferment for flavour, and the rest in the final dough, sized for the time you have [@novita-0077]. See [preferments](/en/learn/preferments) and [retarding dough in the fridge](/en/learn/cold-retard).
