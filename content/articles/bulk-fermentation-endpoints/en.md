Bulk fermentation should stop when the dough holds enough gas and has not yet lost its structure. Stopped too early, the loaf is dense; left too long, the dough turns very sticky because acid and enzymes break down the gluten, and it bakes flat [@breadcode-1470; @breadcode-0860; @breadcode-0444]. The window can be narrow: from one dough split in two, the half given 6 hours had excellent oven spring and the half given 8 hours was flat and sticky [@breadcode-2239]. Judging by look and feel works after many loaves; before that, measure [@breadcode-0495].

## The aliquot jar

An aliquot jar is a small sample of the dough, kept in a jar, whose rise tracks the fermentation of the main dough [@breadcode-0496]. It reads true only under a few conditions.

- **Take it at the right moment.** Sample after the last kneading, so that the starter is evenly spread through the dough [@breadcode-1303].
- **Use a straight jar.** The container must be cylindrical for the rise to be read as a percentage, and a rubber band marks the starting level better than a pen [@breadcode-0506; @breadcode-2890]. In a glass that widens, the same level means more dough: aim lower, about +25 % where a cylinder would read +50 % [@breadcode-2224].
- **Keep it at the dough's temperature.** Leave the jar next to the dough, ideally in the same container [@breadcode-0508]. A small sample heats and cools faster than a large dough, so mix with room-temperature water: after hot water the jar cools first and lags, after cold water it warms first and runs ahead [@breadcode-0507].

The jar has limits. When the room temperature swings during the day, or the dough is warmer than the room, mark a see-through container holding the whole dough instead [@breadcode-0958; @breadcode-1521]. A batch above about 10 kg of flour warms itself as it ferments, and the jar does not [@breadcode-0510]. The sample is not degassed by folds, so one baker saw it run ahead of the folded dough [@breadcode-1680]. And because a small sample changes temperature faster than the dough [@breadcode-0507], the jar is a tool for bulk at room temperature, not for the fridge, where it would cool first.

## How much rise: the flour's strength

*The Sourdough Framework* sets the target by flour protein: +25 % for 8–10 % protein, +50 % for 10–12 %, +100 % for 12–15 %, and more than that above 15 % [@science-ferm-0056; @breadcode-0503]. The reasoning is that the gluten content sets how long a dough tolerates fermentation [@breadcode-0825]. Its advice for a first bake is to stop at +25 %, push further in later bakes and keep the value you like best [@breadcode-0499]. Rye is stopped at about +50 %, and low-gluten flours in general collapse when bulk runs long [@breadcode-0334; @breadcode-0827].

These targets are for sourdough. For a yeasted dough, including an enriched one, the same author aims for a doubling whatever the flour, notes that a slight collapse does no harm [@breadcode-2016], and that a somewhat longer bulk is not critical [@breadcode-2712]. Novita Listyani also takes yeasted doughs to a doubling, read against the midline of the bowl [@novita-0307]. There is a ceiling all the same: in an instrumented test, a dough with 3 % yeast at 30 °C was tallest after two hours and lower after three [@codinagg2013].

## How much rise: what comes next

The Sourdough Journey sets the target by dough temperature instead, for a 20 % starter dough that then spends 12–16 hours in the fridge: +30 % at 27 °C, +50 % at 24 °C, +75 % at 21 °C and +100 % at 18 °C [@science-ferm-0046]. The reason is that a warm dough keeps fermenting through shaping and the first hours of the retard [@science-ferm-0057]. The Bread Code does the same for pizza: bulk is stopped at about +50 % before the cold stage, although the same flour could go to +100 % [@breadcode-1347].

The two rules answer different questions. Flour strength says how much fermentation the gluten can take in total [@breadcode-0825]; the temperature and the schedule say how much is still to come after you stop [@science-ferm-0057]. A dough that goes straight to a warm proof can use the flour's full target; a warm dough headed for a long retard should stop well short of it. [Retarding dough in the fridge](/en/learn/cold-retard) explains what happens next.

## Reading the dough itself

- **Surface.** Bubbles show that the dough is inflated; they multiply as bulk goes on, then vanish when an over-fermented dough starts to leak gas [@breadcode-0530].
- **Smell.** A ripe dough smells like a starter just before it collapses [@breadcode-0531].
- **Touch.** At the right point the dough is tacky but less sticky than earlier; a very sticky dough has gone too far [@breadcode-0534].
- **Acidity.** With a pH meter, the same author ends sourdough bulk at about pH 4.2–4.5, lower for a sourer loaf and higher for a lighter one [@breadcode-1641]. The floor is not far below: in broth, the sourdough lactobacilli stopped growing under about pH 3.9 [@ganzlemg1998].
- **Pace.** In timed doughs at 19–23 °C, going from +75 % to +100 % took one to two hours [@science-ferm-0042], so an hourly check near the end is enough at room temperature. It is the rhythm of the Framework's own flowchart [@breadcode-0560].

## Where the sources disagree

- **Stop early or late when unsure?** The Framework says to lean toward a longer bulk, because a slightly over-fermented loaf tastes better than a pale, under-fermented one [@breadcode-0489]. In his videos the same author says to finish early rather than late, since a longer bulk sours the dough and weakens it [@breadcode-1613; @breadcode-2837]. Both hold: under-fermentation costs flavour, over-fermentation costs shape. With a weak flour, shape is lost first.
- **Double, or less?** "Wait until it has doubled" is right for the 14–15 % protein flour used in two of those videos [@breadcode-1582; @breadcode-1713], and too far for a 10–11 % flour, which the table stops at +50 %. For new bakers the same creator suggests +50 % rather than a doubling [@breadcode-2261].
- **Flour or temperature?** The Framework's table and The Sourdough Journey's guideline give different targets for the same dough at 27 °C: at least +50 % for a flour above 10 % protein, against +30 %. Both are creator rules, not measurements [@science-ferm-0057].

A dough that has gone too far can still be baked in a greased loaf pan, after a rest of 30 to 60 minutes, or used as part of the next starter [@breadcode-0535; @breadcode-0861]. [Troubleshooting the crumb](/en/learn/troubleshooting-crumb) shows what an over-fermented loaf looks like, and [shaping and the final proof](/en/learn/shaping-and-final-proof) covers the step after bulk.

The [fermentation calculator](/en/calculators/fermentation) takes the endpoint as an input (for sourdough, from +25 % to doubled) and returns the likely range of hours, 80 % of bakes: start checking the jar at the early end. [Fermentation time and temperature](/en/learn/fermentation-time-and-temperature) covers the time side.
