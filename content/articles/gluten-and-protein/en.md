Gluten is the network that wheat proteins build once flour meets water. Glutenins, whose long chains are linked by disulphide bonds, give it strength and elasticity; the compact gliadins let it flow and stretch [@cuisine2018; @novita-0278]. Together they make up about 70–80 % of the protein in the wheat grain [@wieser2022]. The network traps the carbon dioxide of fermentation, and the gas cells expand in the oven [@breadcode-0013]. Much of it forms on its own while the dough rests after mixing [@breadcode-0466]; [mixing, rests and folds](/en/learn/mixing-and-gluten) covers the handling.

## Quality, not only quantity

The figure on the bag counts all the protein, and gluten is only part of it: 80–90 % in white flour, 60–80 % in wholemeal, where the outer layers add other proteins [@geisslitz2019].

What the gluten does depends on its make-up. The more gliadin relative to glutenin, the softer and more extensible the dough: the ratio averaged 2.5 in bread wheat and 6.5 in einkorn, and the old wheats carried more protein than bread wheat but a softer, less elastic gluten [@geisslitz2019]. Across 90 spelt varieties, wholemeal protein averaged 18.99 % while the gluten index averaged 59, against 97–100 for two bread-wheat controls [@toth2022]. Strain hardening, the way a stretched film of dough resists further stretching, is considered a requirement for gas retention: good bread flours keep it up to about 60 °C in the oven, poor ones lose it at 45–50 °C, so their gas cells merge earlier and the loaf stays smaller [@vliet2008]. European flours are often sold with their alveograph W, which correlates with bread volume but is only a rough guide [@novita-0185]; [flour types](/en/learn/flour-types) explains it.

Protein still counts. Water absorption rises by about 1 point per 1 % protein [@tipples1978], and a higher-protein flour tolerates a longer fermentation, because proteases, bacteria and acid slowly break gluten down [@breadcode-0408; @breadcode-1427].

## The basis behind the figure

Protein is calculated from nitrogen, and both the factor and the moisture basis change the number. European labels use N × 6.25 on the flour as sold; the Italian legal minima use N × 5.70 on dry matter, so at 14.5 % moisture the 9.00, 11.00 and 12.00 % minima of types 00, 0 and 1 read about 8.4, 10.3 and 11.3 % on a label [@science-flour-0062]. US figures are usually given at 14 % moisture [@novita-0636]. The French food composition table prints both factors: 9.9 % for its T55 with 6.25, 9.03 % with 5.7 [@anses2020]. Put two figures on the same basis before comparing them or using the dose formula below.

## Protein targets by bread

These targets come from creators' rules and two references; read them as the figure on the bag, US values being at 14 % moisture. More is not always better: very strong flour makes steamed buns shrink after steaming [@novita-0776].

| Bread | Protein |
|---|---|
| Steamed buns (mantou) | 10.5–11.5 % [@novita-0772] |
| Lighter fried doughs (youtiao) | all-purpose, about 11–12 % [@novita-0267; @science-flour-0021; @science-flour-0022] |
| Baguette | about 11.5–12 % [@bourre2008; @novita-0380] |
| Doughnuts | 12–13 % [@novita-0256] |
| Free-standing sourdough, long fermentation | at least 12 % [@breadcode-0982] |
| Soft pan loaf | about 13 % [@novita-0845] |
| 100 % whole wheat | at least 13.5 % [@novita-0032] |
| Bagels, chewy hearth breads | about 14 % (one miller's high-gluten flour) [@science-flour-0022] |

## Vital wheat gluten

**What it is.** Vital wheat gluten is concentrated gluten extracted from wheat flour [@breadcode-1104]. Commercial products have roughly 73 % protein (78 % on dry matter), 6 % fat and 6 % moisture, absorb 140–180 % of their weight in water and lose their function if dried above 55 °C [@science-flour-0060]; the USDA lists 75.16 % protein at 8.2 % water [@usdepartmentofagriculture2018]. Use the figure on your own bag.

**Dose.** To raise F grams of flour from protein P_f to a target P_t with a gluten of protein P_g, add

G = F × (P_t − P_f) / (P_g − P_t)

with all three on the same basis and nitrogen factor [@science-flour-0096]. For 1,000 g of flour at 11.0 % brought to 13.0 % with 75 % gluten: G = 1,000 × 2.0 / 62.0 = 32.3 g, and the check gives (1,000 × 11 + 32.3 × 75) / 1,032.3 = 13.0 %; a naive "1 % gluten adds 0.75 point" would suggest 2.7 % of the flour, too little [@science-flour-0096]. If the total must stay at 1,000 g, the same mass balance with gluten replacing flour gives 1,000 × 2.0 / (75 − 11) = 31.25 g: our own arithmetic, not a published dose. On a dry-matter basis (flour at 14 %, gluten at 8.2 % moisture) the answer is 32.6 g [@science-flour-0096]. BAKERpedia's rule of about 0.6 point of protein per 1 % gluten [@science-flour-0060] points the same way: 2.0 / 0.6 is about 3.3 % of the flour, close to the 3.2 % above (our division, not a published dose). The [flour calculator](/en/calculators/flour) works out the dose for a blend, adding the gluten on top of the flour.

**Water.** Add about 1.5 points of dough water per 1 % gluten [@science-flour-0060], within the 1.35–2.15 g per gram that composition tables give for gluten [@science-flour-0053]: about 48 g in the example.

**How much.** Most yeast doughs take 1–4 % of the flour, high-fibre breads up to 12 % [@science-flour-0060]; a French reference rye bread, two-thirds rye, adds 1 % [@science-flour-0007]. One creator suggests 5 g per 100 g of a low-gluten flour [@breadcode-1105], above the usual range.

**Too much.** No source measured an overdose of vital gluten. The nearest warnings concern a very strong flour, which gives a too-strong crumb in a soft loaf [@novita-0845], and missing water: gluten added dry takes its 140–180 % from the dough [@science-flour-0060], and a dough short of water turns stiff, tears and holds little gas [@novita-0649].

## Whole grain and rye: where the figure misleads

Bran brings protein that is not gluten [@geisslitz2019], and creators ask at least 13.5 % for 100 % whole wheat against 12 % for a white free-standing loaf [@novita-0032; @breadcode-0982]. US whole wheat flour lists 13.2 % protein against 10.3 % for all-purpose [@usdepartmentofagriculture2018], yet one creator finds that whole wheat dough carries more enzymes and runs faster [@breadcode-1501]. Whole wheat flours from cooler climates often have less protein, so test each new one [@breadcode-1265]. In one trial, a 100 % whole wheat dough at very high hydration held together only after the creator added 70 g of vital gluten to 450 g of flour [@breadcode-1710; @breadcode-1748].

Rye is the extreme case. Pooled French T130 rye had 9.1 % protein [@bourre2008], and American dark rye, a clear flour, lists 15.9 % [@science-flour-0102; @usdepartmentofagriculture2018], but neither builds a gluten network: rye's structure comes from pentosans and acid-protected starch [@science-flour-0098]. [Rye, spelt and other grains](/en/learn/rye-spelt-and-other-grains) covers these flours.
