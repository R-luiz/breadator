A preferment is a portion of the dough's ingredients fermented before the final dough is mixed [@breadcode-0868]. It lengthens the overall fermentation, adds flavour and allows some gluten breakdown without weakening the whole dough [@novita-0653]: during the long rest, enzymes and yeast split proteins and starch into amino acids and sugars that feed flavour [@novita-0497]. A dough made with a poolish also mixes faster and is more extensible [@novita-0167]. A yeasted preferment makes few organic acids, and acidifies mainly through dissolved CO2 [@novita-0295; @novita-0300], so it tastes yeasty rather than sour [@breadcode-2793]. This is where a yeasted bread gets flavour; the 15–30 minutes of an autolyse are too short for much enzyme work [@calvel2001].

## The family

Poolish, biga and sponge are the same thing at different hydrations [@breadcode-2799].

| Preferment | Make-up | Points to know |
|---|---|---|
| Poolish | equal weights of flour and water, a little yeast [@breadcode-1822] | ferments faster than a biga [@novita-0758] |
| Biga | stiff, about 50 % hydration | harder to mix evenly [@novita-0309] |
| Sponge | a share of the flour with yeast | in industry: 70 % of the flour for 3–4 h, or a liquid sponge with 20–50 % for 1–2 h [@novita-0302] |
| Refrigerated sponge | a sponge fermented in the fridge | half the flour for 18 h in one whole-wheat study [@zeng2023] |
| Pâte fermentée | a piece of the previous dough, salt included | no separate culture to keep [@breadcode-2576] |

## How much flour

Reported shares run from 20 % of the total flour in a sandwich loaf [@breadcode-1822] through about 40 % in mini baguettes [@novita-0702] to an upper limit of 50 % [@novita-0597]. The preferment's water is part of the dough's water: a dough at 75 % in the final mix came to about 79 % with its poolish [@breadcode-2178]. [Baker's percentages](/en/learn/bakers-percentages) shows how to count it.

## Yeast dose, time and temperature

A French professional table gives the fresh yeast per kg of poolish flour for a ripening time at 22–24 °C [@science-ferm-0008]:

| Time to ripe | Fresh yeast per kg of poolish flour | As instant yeast (one third) |
|---|---|---|
| 2 h | 20–25 g | 0.7–0.8 % |
| 4 h | 15 g | 0.5 % |
| 7 h | 7 g | 0.23 % |
| 12 h | 4 g | 0.13 % |
| 16 h | 2 g | 0.07 % |
| 18 h | 1 g | 0.03 % |

A home-baking schedule agrees: 0.23–0.33 % instant yeast for up to 8 hours, 0.1–0.2 % for 12 and 0.03–0.08 % for 16, the low figure in summer [@science-ferm-0010]. A second professional table gives almost the same doses and times (0.8 % fresh yeast for 7–8 hours, 0.25 % for 12–15) but for 26–29 °C, 4–5 °C warmer [@science-ferm-0009]. Either one temperature is loosely stated or the two do not mean the same thing by "ripe". Ripeness is in any case well past doubling: across 48 table and recipe rows, a ripe poolish took about 1.9 times as long as the same dose needs to double a dough [@science-ferm-0011]. [Commercial yeast](/en/learn/commercial-yeast) explains the conversion between fresh and instant yeast, and the [yeast converter](/en/calculators/yeast) does it for you.

Creators' recipes span a wide range. Novita Listyani's poolish with 1 % yeast peaks in about 3.5 hours at 27 °C [@novita-0592], and another of her instant-yeast poolishes matures in 5 to 8 hours at 29 °C [@novita-0458]. Her usual routine is 1–2 hours at 27–29 °C, then a night at 5–10 °C [@novita-0594], with a very small dose so that the poolish does not over-ferment in a warm room [@novita-0157]. The Bread Code's poolish holds 2 % dry yeast on its flour and waits about 10 hours overnight [@breadcode-2176; @breadcode-2177], or about 24 hours in another video, where it was judged ready once doubled and bubbly [@breadcode-2797; @breadcode-2800]. By the tables above, that much yeast is ripe within two hours, so these poolishes wait well past their peak by that measure; the professional tables instead match the dose to the time. Adding yeast to shorten a poolish works, but the same author does not recommend it [@breadcode-2802].

## Ripe

A ripe poolish is covered in bubbles and its surface has just turned slightly concave: it has risen as far as it will and begins to recede [@novita-0446; @novita-0758]. Novita also uses the float test; past that point the poolish collapses and loses strength [@novita-0345]. In the fridge, look for a bubbly, wobbly mass that has sunk a little after peaking [@novita-0580].

## Into the final dough

A preferment kept cold lowers the dough temperature (see [dough temperature](/en/learn/dough-temperature)), which helps in a warm room [@novita-0493], and adds about 2–3 minutes of mixing [@novita-0401]. A poolish that spent the night in the fridge is there for flavour more than for gas, so extra yeast goes into the final dough [@novita-0765]; that second dose is the one to adjust to your schedule [@novita-0077]. With an active poolish plus yeast, bulk is short: about 30 minutes to 1.5 times the volume in a warm room in one recipe [@novita-0171], one to two hours to a doubling in another [@breadcode-2184]. Novita considers a cold ferment of the final dough unnecessary after a poolish, even counterproductive [@novita-0596].

## Pâte fermentée

Cut the piece from the finished dough at the end of bulk, when it is ready to shape, and mix it straight into the next dough [@breadcode-2665]. Its salt and its hydration should match the dough it will seed [@breadcode-2577; @breadcode-2578]. The Bread Code's doses are about 10 % of the flour for a bake the next day, 5 % for a bake in a week and 2.5 % for a bake in a month [@breadcode-2664]. Refrigerating the reserve slows its microbes; kept at room temperature, it should be used within one to three days [@breadcode-2581]. The method serves sourdough and, according to the same author, yeasted doughs too [@breadcode-2964].

## Refrigerated sponge

In the whole-wheat study, half of the flour was fermented with yeast for 18 hours in the fridge before the final dough. The bread was softer than both the straight-dough and the sourdough versions and its gluten network was stronger; its resistant starch reached 13.8 %, against 7.7 % for the straight dough and 14.75 % for the sourdough version, which was not significantly different from the sponge; it also browned less [@zeng2023]. A home version puts a whole-wheat poolish with about 1.2 % instant yeast straight into the fridge for 18 hours [@novita-0035].
