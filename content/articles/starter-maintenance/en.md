A mature starter is a small dough without salt. Left unfed, it fills with acid and the bacteria take over; a feed dilutes the acid, brings fresh food and restores the yeast activity [@breadcode-0362]. Discarding part of it before each feed keeps its size and its acidity in check [@novita-0210]. To grow one from flour and water, see [Making a sourdough starter](/en/learn/sourdough-starter).

## Feeding ratio and time to peak

A ratio such as 1:5:5 means 1 part starter, 5 parts flour and 5 parts water by weight. The more flour and water per part of starter, the later the peak, which lets you fit the feed to your day [@breadcode-0369]. A larger feed also leaves less acid in the ripe starter [@breadcode-1426].

The table gives model estimates for a 100 % hydration starter of average vigour [@science-ferm-0062]. Treat them as starting points: the published statements they were checked against sit within a factor of about 1.4 [@science-ferm-0060; @science-ferm-0061; @science-ferm-0063]. The [starter feeding calculator](/en/calculators/starter) gives the same estimate for any feed, temperature and starter hydration, or the feed that peaks at the time you want.

| Feed (starter:flour:water) | Peak at 21 °C | Peak at 24 °C |
|---|---|---|
| 1:1:1 | about 4 h | about 3 h |
| 1:2:2 | about 6 h | about 4½ h |
| 1:5:5 | about 9 h | about 6½ h |
| 1:10:10 | about 11½ h | about 8½ h |

Bakers' own figures agree roughly. *The Sourdough Framework* feeds 1:5:5 and makes the dough 6–12 hours later [@breadcode-0357]. One practitioner saw 1:1:1 and 1:2:2 feeds peak in 3.5–6 hours at room temperature [@science-ferm-0063]. Not everything fits: Novita Listyani, who builds her levain at 1:2:2, waits about 6 hours at 29 °C for it to more than double [@novita-0643; @novita-0843], longer than the model predicts, and one practitioner finds a 1:2:2 feed only about 15 % slower than 1:1:1, where the model says nearly 50 % [@science-ferm-0060]. Note your own starter's peak times: they are the best calibration you can get.

Temperature works both ways. In a cold kitchen, feed with warm water [@breadcode-0283]; in a warm one, the Framework feeds larger and stiffer, 1:10:5 or 1:20:10 [@breadcode-0368]. Warmer is not always better: in the study that measured both, the sourdough yeast grew fastest near 27 °C and the lactobacilli near 32–33 °C, so above about 30 °C a starter may sour before it has risen fully [@ganzlemg1998].

Which ratio to use for routine feeds is disputed. The Framework feeds 1:5:5 or 1:10:10 and rejects 1:1:1 [@breadcode-0895; @breadcode-0365]. Novita maintains at 1:1:1, fed every 12–24 hours, and builds her levain at 1:2:2 [@novita-0208; @novita-0209; @novita-0643]. In one of her side-by-side jars (a single trial at 26–30 °C), a 1:1:1 feed had doubled by about 5 hours and looked ready before it had actually peaked [@novita-0454].

## Stiff, regular or liquid

Starter types differ only in the water given at each feed: about 100 % of the flour weight for a regular starter, 50–60 % for a stiff one, about 500 % for the Framework's liquid starter [@breadcode-0245].

- **Stiff** (feed 1:5:2.5). It should feel like pasta dough, with no dry flour left [@breadcode-0367; @breadcode-0210]. The Framework rates it the most yeast-active and, for the same rise, the least acidic; in the author's own balloon test, with only the hydration changed, the stiff starter made the most gas and the liquid one the least [@breadcode-0219; @breadcode-0243; @breadcode-0221]. Dissolve it in the dough water before mixing [@breadcode-0232].
- **Liquid** (feed 1:5:25). The layer of water limits oxygen, the vinegar note fades and a yoghurt-like one replaces it [@breadcode-0197; @breadcode-0193]. The Framework treats the change as permanent, so keep a backup of the original starter first [@breadcode-0196]. It needs a very strong flour [@breadcode-0202].

A change of type shows after one feed and is stronger after two or three daily feeds [@breadcode-0174].

The published evidence does not match the Framework here. Four traditional sourdoughs were kept for 28 days either firm (dough yield 160, about 60 % hydration) or liquid (dough yield 280, about 180 %). The firm ones ended with more lactic and acetic acid; the liquid ones had the highest yeast counts and the lowest acidity [@dicagnor2014]. That "liquid" is much thicker than a 500 % starter, and the study counted cells rather than gas, so the results are not strictly comparable. Judge a stiff starter by what it does in your own dough.

## Which flour to feed

The Bread Code feeds whole-grain flour (rye, spelt, wheat, emmer or einkorn), on the grounds that the nutrients and microbes sit in the outer layers of the grain [@breadcode-1564]. Its author also rotates between whole grains from one feed to the next, which he says adds microbial variety [@breadcode-1565]. In one pressure-vessel test of 1:1:1 feeds at 20 °C, whole wheat gave almost twice the gas of bread flour or rye in the first hours, but by 36 hours bread flour had made more [@science-ferm-0034]. Each feed selects microbes suited to the flour in use [@calvertmd2021], hence the Framework's advice: treat the starter as a small version of the dough it will raise, and keep it in the same conditions [@breadcode-0899].

## Fridge, freezer, drying

| Next bake | Storage | To bring it back |
|---|---|---|
| Within about 2 weeks | Fridge at 4 °C | Feed 1:5:5, wait 8–12 h |
| Later | Freezer, or dried with flour | Feed 1:5:5 daily for about 3 days |

The table follows the Framework [@breadcode-0151; @breadcode-0152; @breadcode-0162; @breadcode-0163; @breadcode-0157]. A thin layer of water over a refrigerated starter stops its surface from drying [@breadcode-0153]. A fully dried starter keeps for years [@breadcode-0161].

How long a starter can stay in the fridge, and how much reviving it needs afterwards, depends on whom you ask. Novita feeds a refrigerated starter once a week and gives it one or two feeds at room temperature before baking [@novita-0212]. The Framework draws its line at three days without food: within that, one 1:5:5 feed 6–12 hours ahead; beyond it, two 1:10:10 feeds, about 48 hours and 6–12 hours ahead [@breadcode-0357; @breadcode-0358]. Bread Code videos speak of a month in the fridge, then about two days of feeding [@breadcode-1566; @breadcode-1568]. In a single measured run, a starter kept 7 days in the fridge peaked about 2 hours later than a fresh one after the same 1:1:1 feed [@science-ferm-0059].

The fridge slows a starter; whether it stops it is another disagreement. One video says fermentation halts at 4 °C [@breadcode-1566]. A guest baker on the same channel says a cold starter keeps going and eventually collapses [@breadcode-1685]. In broth, one fit puts the minimum growth temperature at 3–4 °C for the sourdough lactobacilli and at 8 °C for the yeast, though its authors call it poor near the minimum [@ganzlemg1998]; on that reading, a fridge at 4 °C would let the bacteria creep on while the yeast stands still.

The other route is to skip the feed and use a much smaller dose of the old starter, leaving the culture to regrow in the dough. The Framework still rates feeding as the more reliable of the two [@breadcode-0378].

## Ready to use

- **Look and smell.** Bubbles along the glass, a risen surface, a smell of yoghurt or mild vinegar [@breadcode-0186; @breadcode-0361]. A stiff starter domes and smells more of alcohol [@breadcode-0226].
- **Your own rise mark.** Use the starter just before it peaks and falls; depending on the flour, that may be +50 %, +100 % or +200 % [@breadcode-0891].
- **Early beats late.** A little early is the safer side; past the peak, use less starter in the dough (the Framework's example cuts a 20 % dose to 10 %) [@breadcode-0892; @breadcode-0893].

How much ripe starter goes into the dough, fresh or starving, is the subject of [Levain and inoculation](/en/learn/levain-and-inoculation). The [fermentation calculator](/en/calculators/fermentation) turns that dose and your dough temperature into a time.
