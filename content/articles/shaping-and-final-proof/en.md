Shaping gives the loaf its form and its tension, and it presses out much of the gas made during the bulk fermentation. The final proof then inflates the dough again; the chemistry and biology are the same as in the bulk [@breadcode-0586; @breadcode-0585].

## Dividing and preshaping

Dividing and preshaping are needed when one batch makes several loaves, and optional for a single loaf [@breadcode-0549]. Weigh the pieces so that they match [@breadcode-0550]. Cutting leaves torn, uneven pieces; preshaping rounds each into a smooth base for the final shape, adds strength to a weak dough and evens out the crumb [@breadcode-1267; @breadcode-0553].

It has a cost. Rounding degasses the dough, and skipping it gives a more open crumb [@breadcode-1021]. *The Sourdough Framework* limits it to about five movements, working down towards three, and warns that an over-preshaped dough may not recover [@breadcode-0556; @breadcode-0484]. In his videos, the same author suggests avoiding the preshape altogether where possible: one dough, one container, shaped directly [@breadcode-1840].

Preshape on a bench without flour. The dough has to grip the surface for the tension to form: push it with a scraper at about 45° and drag, and the top pulls taut [@breadcode-1268; @breadcode-2650]. If the skin tears, stop, wait about 15 minutes and start again [@breadcode-2618].

## Bench rest

After preshaping the gluten is tight. The rest lets it relax, so that the dough does not spring back during the final shape; without it, a long shape such as a baguette cannot be formed [@breadcode-0558].

| Source | Bench rest |
|---|---|
| The Sourdough Framework | 10–15 min at least, up to about 30 min if the dough resists |
| Bread Code videos | usually about 15 min; from 5–10 to about 50 min |
| Novita Listyani | 20 min for small buns, 30–45 min for baguettes |

The figures come from the Framework [@breadcode-0557; @breadcode-0559], the videos [@breadcode-1372; @breadcode-1953; @breadcode-1971] and Novita's recipes [@novita-0790; @novita-0704]. The spread says that the clock is the wrong measure. Rest until the dough has relaxed and flattened a little [@breadcode-2231], but not so long that the surface dries and tears [@breadcode-0559]. The Framework leaves the pieces uncovered, because a slightly dry surface sticks less to the hands [@breadcode-0557].

## The final shape: tension

A loaf rises upwards in the oven only if its surface is under tension [@breadcode-1110]. The tighter the shaping, the more even the crumb and the more vertical the oven spring [@breadcode-0562; @breadcode-0563]. Tension does not replace strength built earlier, though: the Framework calls tighter shaping the last option for a weak dough [@breadcode-1063].

- **Flour.** Use it only now, and as little as possible: too much keeps the dough from tucking and rounding properly [@breadcode-2465]. Flour the top and flip the dough: the sticky side now facing you is the glue for the folds [@breadcode-0566; @breadcode-0568].
- **A batard.** Stretch the round into a rectangle, fold the near side to the middle, fold the far side over it, turn, then roll it up from the top [@breadcode-0569; @breadcode-0572; @breadcode-0573]. Stitching the seam and rolling both add strength [@breadcode-2652]; sealing the seam harder only improves the look of the bottom [@breadcode-0583].
- **Too tight.** If the surface tears, rest the dough 15 minutes and reshape [@breadcode-1469].
- **Sticky or tearing dough.** It is probably over-fermented: move it to a loaf pan [@breadcode-0576]. See [Troubleshooting the crumb](/en/learn/troubleshooting-crumb).

Rub the surface with flour once more, then place the loaf in the banneton with the smooth floured side down and the seam up; a bowl lined with a towel also works [@breadcode-0578; @breadcode-0581; @breadcode-0816]. The sources split on which flour. The Framework uses the flour of the dough and keeps rice flour for decorative scoring [@breadcode-0579]. Videos recommend rice flour because it absorbs moisture and prevents sticking [@breadcode-1863]. The book's troubleshooting chapter adds that a dough which sticks to the banneton is over-fermented, and that rice flour only hides it [@breadcode-1006].

## Proofing at room temperature

There is no fixed time: the proof depends on the temperature and on the amount and activity of the starter or yeast [@breadcode-2242]. [Fermentation time](/en/learn/fermentation-time-and-temperature) shows how temperature and dose set the clock.

- The Framework gives 30 minutes to 3 hours; its author has never seen less than 1 hour, even in warm weather [@breadcode-0598; @breadcode-0604].
- A Bread Code video gives 1–3 hours for sourdough and 30 minutes to 2 hours for yeast dough [@breadcode-1642].
- Novita's yeasted recipes range from 30–45 minutes for boules to 2–3 hours for a shokupan, judged by its height in the pan [@novita-0528; @novita-0385].

How far to go also varies. The Framework proofs a pan loaf until it has roughly doubled [@breadcode-0306]. Novita stops some loaves before a full doubling, to leave room for the oven spring [@novita-0521]. At the end of the proof gas makes up about 70–75 % of the dough's volume, and the dough is fragile [@sun2023]. Both creators would rather stop slightly early than late [@breadcode-0607; @novita-0691]. Preheat the oven when the dough is close, so that it does not overproof while the oven heats [@breadcode-0605].

## The poke test

Flour a finger and press 0.5–1 cm into the dough [@breadcode-0599]. Just after shaping, the dent fills back within about a minute. As the dough fills with gas it becomes more extensible, and the dent recovers more slowly [@breadcode-0600; @breadcode-0601]. The dough is ready when the dent is still visible after one minute; test about every 15 minutes [@breadcode-0602; @breadcode-0603].

| The dent | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Springs back at once | Wait |
| Recovers slowly, leaves a mark | Ready |
| Does not recover; the dough feels sticky | Over-proofed: bake now |

The readings are those of the videos and of Novita [@breadcode-1473; @novita-0711; @breadcode-1797].

### Its limits

- **Cold dough.** The test does not work on dough from the fridge: cold changes its elasticity and the dent never recovers [@breadcode-0613; @breadcode-1307]. See [Retarding dough in the fridge](/en/learn/cold-retard).
- **Scalded and very wet doughs.** Tangzhong and yudane doughs hold gas less well and need a longer proof; Novita judges them by eye, not by the poke [@novita-0373]. At very high hydration the test can mislead [@novita-0756]. See [Tangzhong, yudane and other scalds](/en/learn/tangzhong-yudane-scalds).
- **It is a judgement.** The depth alone differs between texts: 0.5–1 cm in the Framework's chapter, about 13 mm in its glossary [@breadcode-0599; @breadcode-0839].
- **It reads the proof only.** According to the Framework, an under-fermented loaf usually goes back to the bulk fermentation or to the starter, not to the final proof [@breadcode-1047]; see [Bulk fermentation: knowing when to stop](/en/learn/bulk-fermentation-endpoints).

Other marks help: the height of the dough in the pan, a volume of about 1.5 to 2 times for buns [@novita-0791; @novita-0769], or the pH at the end of the proofs that gave your best loaves [@breadcode-0619].

When the dough is ready, see [Baking and steam](/en/learn/baking-and-steam). The [fermentation calculator](/en/calculators/fermentation) estimates the time for your dough temperature.
