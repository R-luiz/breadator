An enriched dough adds sugar, fat, milk or eggs to flour, water, salt and yeast. Each softens the crumb or adds flavour; sugar slows the yeast and fat slows the gluten [@science-ferm-0030; @novita-0853].

## Four families

| Bread | Sugar | Fat | Other |
|---|---|---|---|
| Milk bread, shokupan | 6–8 % | about 6 % in total | often a tangzhong on 20 % of the flour |
| Sweet buns, burger buns | 10–16 % | about 10 % butter | milk as the liquid, sometimes egg |
| Brioche-style buns | 10 % | 20 % butter | milk, eggs |
| Croissant | 10 % | about 60 % butter, 50 % of it laminated | half milk, half water |

All figures are percentages of the flour weight, taken from Novita Listyani's recipes and from The Bread Code's [@novita-0352; @novita-0486; @novita-0015; @novita-0820; @novita-0822; @breadcode-1155; @breadcode-2545; @breadcode-2827; @breadcode-2829]. For the scald, see [Tangzhong, yudane and other scalds](/en/learn/tangzhong-yudane-scalds).

## Sugar: food, then brake

Up to about 5–6 % of the flour, sugar raises gas production; beyond that, osmotic pressure slows the yeast [@science-ferm-0030; @novita-0483]. Sugar binds water: a dough with 18 % sugar and 45 % water had a water activity of 0.95, against 0.97 with 6 % sugar and 52 % water [@aslankoohie2015]. In a yeast manufacturer's figure, an ordinary lean-dough yeast keeps about 80 % of its activity at 10 % sugar, 60 % at 20 % and 40 % at 30 % [@science-ferm-0029].

For a soft white loaf, Novita keeps sugar near 6 %, citing studies she does not name, and 6–8 % by experience [@novita-0762; @novita-0352]. Sugar also browns: she bakes sugar-rich doughs at about 170 °C and covers the top with paper if it darkens early [@novita-0326].

When to add it depends on the fermentation. *The Sourdough Framework* adds sugar at shaping, because in a long sourdough fermentation the microbes eat it: after about 12 hours no sweetness is left [@breadcode-0741; @breadcode-0293]. Novita's yeasted doughs take their sugar at mixing and ferment for an hour or two in all [@novita-0753; @novita-0403]. The two rules apply to different time scales.

## Osmotolerant yeast: when it is worth it

Osmotolerant yeast is a strain that tolerates high sugar, helped by the glycerol and trehalose it accumulates [@novita-0625]. On when to switch, the sources differ.

- **The manufacturer.** Raise the yeast dose once sugar exceeds 10 % [@science-ferm-0022]. In its figure, the sugar-tolerant strain is the weaker one in a lean dough, crosses the lean-dough yeast near 10 % sugar and is clearly ahead at 20 % [@science-ferm-0029].
- **Novita.** At 16 % sugar, ordinary instant yeast still does the job; the fermentation is only a little longer [@novita-0820]. Above that she adds about 1 g of yeast per recipe [@novita-0323].

A working rule that fits both: under 10 % sugar, any yeast; from 10 to about 16 %, ordinary yeast with more time or a slightly higher dose, or osmotolerant yeast; above that, osmotolerant yeast or a larger dose [@science-ferm-0029; @novita-0820; @novita-0323]. The [yeast converter](/en/calculators/yeast) converts between yeast types ([Commercial yeast](/en/learn/commercial-yeast) explains why the factors differ), and the [fermentation calculator](/en/calculators/fermentation) applies a sugar and a fat factor to its times and suggests osmotolerant yeast above about 10 % sugar.

## Fat: tender crumb, slower gluten

Fat coats the gluten-forming proteins and competes with water for the flour. The gluten forms more slowly and the crumb becomes finer [@novita-0853]. In small amounts it also lifts the loaf: about 5 % solid fat gives the largest volume in white pan bread, more changes little, and a great deal holds back the proof, which is why a brioche with over 50 % butter is dense [@novita-0830]. Solid fats do this better than oils, because their crystals sit on the surface of the gas cells and stabilize them [@smith2005; @brooker1996]. Oil gives a moister crumb but a lower, denser loaf [@novita-0833].

**Timing.** Add the butter after the gluten has started to form: mix to a cohesive dough, add soft butter, then mix on to a windowpane [@novita-0356]. For a brioche, add it in portions and let each disappear before the next [@novita-0463]. Cold butter in portions keeps a rich dough cool through its long mix [@novita-0462]. [Mixing, rests and folds](/en/learn/mixing-and-gluten) covers the windowpane.

**Flour and water.** A richer dough needs a stronger flour; about 13 % protein is Novita's middle ground for a soft loaf [@novita-0335; @novita-0845]. Butter contains water, so count it in the hydration [@novita-0855]; the [formula calculator](/en/calculators/formula) does this for butter, milk and eggs.

## Milk and eggs

How much milk is disputed. Novita, following a paper she quotes without naming, replaces no more than about 25 % of the water with milk: up to that point elasticity and chewiness improved, and beyond it quality fell [@novita-0821; @novita-0662]. Her own tangzhong, though, can be made with milk alone [@novita-0838]. The Bread Code's bun recipes use milk as the only liquid, at 45–55 % of the flour [@breadcode-1155; @breadcode-2545].

Whole egg strengthens the dough. The white adds strength and lengthens the mix; the yolk shortens it and lowers stability [@novita-0250]. Eggs vary in size and are mostly water, so weigh them [@novita-0800].

## Fermentation and proof

A sweet dough is slower: at 16 % sugar it still has to roughly double, and that takes longer than for a lean dough [@novita-0824]. A yeast-raised enriched dough can go to a full doubling in the first fermentation whatever the flour [@breadcode-2016]. It can be retarded in the fridge for about 12 hours; a butter-rich brioche firms up for shaping in 12–24 hours, and in one batch Novita found the gluten weaker and the taste slightly sour beyond 24 hours [@novita-0336; @novita-0464]. [Retarding dough in the fridge](/en/learn/cold-retard) covers rich doughs in the cold.

With sourdough, a hybrid is the usual route. In The Bread Code's buns the starter is there for flavour and the yeast does most of the lifting [@breadcode-2546]; Novita adds about 0.2 % yeast to a sourdough shokupan when the levain is weak [@novita-0384].

## Sizing the dough for the pan

Novita's method is to work from the volume of the pan [@novita-0230]:

1. Measure the pan: fill it with water and weigh it [@novita-0229].
2. Multiply by a dough density. For her lidded shokupan pan, 257–286 g of dough per litre worked; a 1.5 litre pan would take about 385–430 g [@novita-0478].
3. Scale the recipe to that dough weight from its baker's percentages [@novita-0230]; [Baker's percentages](/en/learn/bakers-percentages) shows how.

The density is hers, for one pan and one dough: treat it as a first try and correct it after a bake. Proof a lidded loaf until the dough is about 3 cm below the rim, and an open one until it stands a little above it [@novita-0385; @novita-0664].

## Egg wash and bake

An egg wash browns through the Maillard reaction. The Bread Code uses one egg with its weight of water, and less water for a darker crust [@breadcode-2554]. Novita adds a spoonful of milk instead: the egg supplies amino acids and the milk lactose, a reducing sugar, so it browns more [@novita-0173].

Oven settings differ too. Novita bakes milk bread and shokupan at 180–190 °C, part of the time with bottom heat only [@novita-0400; @novita-0386]. The Bread Code's burger buns bake at 230 °C for about 20 minutes, with water sprayed during the first 10 [@breadcode-1157]. A loaf in a pan needs the gentler heat: its edges heat slowly, and the top would overcook before the centre is done [@breadcode-0309]. [Baking and steam](/en/learn/baking-and-steam) covers the oven side.
