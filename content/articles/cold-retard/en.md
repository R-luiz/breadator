Retarding means slowing fermentation by putting the dough somewhere colder, usually a refrigerator, so that the baker chooses when the bread is ready [@breadcode-0873]. It applies to the whole dough (cold bulk) or to the shaped loaf (cold proof), typically at 3–5 °C [@novita-0072].

## Slowed, not stopped

*The Sourdough Framework* says fermentation comes to a halt below about 4 °C, with the caveat that the threshold depends on the culture and that a dough cannot stay cold forever [@science-ferm-0082]. Measurements point to "very slow" instead of "stopped". A commercial baker's yeast in a sugar solution still produced over 40 mL of CO2 per gram after 6–7 days at 3–5 °C [@science-ferm-0018]. In broth, the minimum growth temperature was about 8 °C for a sourdough yeast and 3–4 °C for its lactobacilli [@ganzlemg1998]. A blend of those growth curves puts sourdough activity at 3 % of its 25 °C rate at 4 °C and 11 % at 10 °C [@science-ferm-0040], and one practitioner rates 24 hours at 3 °C at no more than 30 minutes of bulk at its peak rate [@science-ferm-0068].

## The cooling hours count most

A dough does not reach fridge temperature when the door closes. It first ferments as fast as at room temperature and slows as it cools [@breadcode-0609]. A 650 g shaped loaf at 25.6 °C, placed in a fridge set to 1.7 °C, read 20.6 °C at its core after 1 hour, 13.9 °C after 2, 9.4 °C after 4, 6.1 °C after 6 and 4.4 °C after 8 [@science-ferm-0064]. Integrated along such a curve, a 12-hour retard of a 26 °C loaf is worth about 1.9 hours of fermentation at 25 °C, of which 1.5 hours happen during the cool-down; a dough at 4 °C from the first minute would get 0.4 hours [@science-ferm-0067].

Everything that changes the cool-down therefore changes the result:

- **Starting temperature.** A warmer dough takes longer to cool and proofs more overnight [@breadcode-0616; @breadcode-2040]. At 30 °C an overnight retard can overproof it [@breadcode-1095]. A dough that goes in cold stops almost at once and may need a short proof at room temperature first [@breadcode-1044].
- **Size and container.** Scaled from the one measured loaf, the time for the gap to the fridge to shrink by about two thirds is roughly 1.8 hours for 200 g of dough, 2.6 hours for 500 g, 3.3 hours for 1 kg and 4.4 hours for 2 kg in a lined banneton inside a bag, and about half that for pieces left bare on a tray in a fan-assisted fridge. These are estimates, not measurements [@science-ferm-0066].
- **How far bulk went.** This is why one practitioner stops bulk at +30 % when the dough is at 27 °C and lets it reach +100 % at 18 °C before the same 12–16 hours in the fridge [@science-ferm-0046]. See [bulk fermentation endpoints](/en/learn/bulk-fermentation-endpoints).

## The fridge itself

Fridges differ, and so do their shelves [@breadcode-2962]. The Bread Code's rule is to stay below 6 °C, because above it the dough keeps fermenting and turns sticky [@breadcode-1322], and to allow about 16 hours at 6 °C or 24 hours at 4 °C [@breadcode-1584]. A fridge measured at about 9 °C keeps proofing the dough [@breadcode-2069], and a loaf whose core stayed near 9 °C came out flat [@breadcode-1733]. Bakery dough fridges typically run at 8–10 °C, and near 10 °C they shorten the final fermentation [@breadcode-1682]. A thermometer on the shelf you use is worth more than the dial.

## How long

For a shaped sourdough loaf, the Framework gives a window of 6 to 24 hours [@breadcode-0594], reports loaves still good after 48 hours [@breadcode-0611], and suggests finding your own time by steps: 8 hours, then 10, then 12, up to 24 [@breadcode-0614]. In his videos the same author either refrigerates right after shaping for 18–24 hours, or proofs at room temperature until the loaf almost passes the poke test and then gives it about 8 hours cold [@breadcode-1908; @breadcode-2256]. Once the loaf is cold the timing is forgiving, an hour or two either way [@breadcode-1936]. At 36 hours one baker found his loaves very sour and considers 24 hours already long [@breadcode-1684].

The finger-poke test (see [shaping and the final proof](/en/learn/shaping-and-final-proof)) does not work on cold dough, whose dent never springs back [@breadcode-0613]. Bake straight from the fridge [@breadcode-1408]; the cold dough is firmer and easier to score [@breadcode-2140].

Whole doughs can be held longer. For pizza, The Bread Code stops bulk at about +50 %, then keeps the dough cold for up to 7 days and takes it out about 3 hours before shaping [@breadcode-1320; @breadcode-1323]. Yeasted doughs in the cold are hard to predict: published fridge schedules scatter by a factor of about 2.2 around a dose-and-temperature law [@science-ferm-0081], and in one study a dough with 6 % fresh yeast reached its target height in about 3 hours at 5 °C [@birch2013b]. [Commercial yeast](/en/learn/commercial-yeast) discusses the dose.

## What it changes in the bread

The first benefit is the schedule: a loaf shaped at 22:00 is baked the next morning [@breadcode-0591]. On flavour the sources differ. The Framework's author could not taste a difference in his retarded loaves [@breadcode-0589]. In a study on yeasted bread, crumb fermented at 5 °C held fewer lipid-oxidation compounds, which are linked to off-flavours, than crumb fermented at 15 or 35 °C, and more fruity esters [@birch2013b]. In sourdough (see [flavour and acidity](/en/learn/sourdough-flavour-and-acidity)) the cold slows acid production; a retarded loaf is more acidic only because it ferments longer [@xu2020]. Long cold fermentation of a yeasted dough can also limit the Maillard aromas of the crust, although longer fermentation raises malty aldehydes [@zehentbauer1998].

On volume, two single side-by-side bakes found slightly more oven spring after a cold proof and no clearly better crumb [@breadcode-1263; @breadcode-2002], and cold fermentation alone has not been shown to raise loaf volume [@gomez2007].

## Rich doughs

An [enriched](/en/learn/enriched-doughs) yeasted dough can be retarded for up to about 12 hours and keeps rising, only more slowly [@novita-0336]. For a high-butter brioche, 12 to 24 hours firm the dough for shaping and build flavour; beyond 24 hours the gluten weakened and the bread turned slightly sour [@novita-0464]. Expect little visible rise: laminated sourdough croissants barely expanded during a night in the cold and then needed about five hours at room temperature to double [@breadcode-1632; @breadcode-1631]. With sourdough, keep chilled rests short, because acid keeps building in the fridge [@breadcode-1627]. No measured cooling or gas data for rich doughs were found; plan a warm final proof and judge it by the dough.

The [fermentation calculator](/en/calculators/fermentation) follows the dough through room, fridge and room phases, cool-down included, and widens its range when most of the time is spent in the cold.
