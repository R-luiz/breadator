The sourness of sourdough bread comes from two acids made by the bacteria of the starter: lactic acid, with a mild dairy note, and acetic acid, the sharp acid of vinegar [@breadcode-0068; @breadcode-0905]. How sour a loaf tastes depends on how much of each is made and how much is still there after the bake. Both can be steered.

## Two acids, two tastes

Homofermentative lactic acid bacteria make lactic acid only. Heterofermentative ones make lactic acid plus acetic acid, ethanol and carbon dioxide [@breadcode-0070; @breadcode-0071; @lau2021]. Lactic acid is chemically the stronger of the two, yet it is often not perceived as sour; acetic acid tastes sharper than its chemistry suggests [@breadcode-0980; @breadcode-0984]. A pH reading therefore does not tell you how a loaf will taste. The nose helps: yoghurt for lactic acid; vinegar, glue or acetone for acetic acid [@breadcode-0128]. *The Sourdough Framework* holds that acetic acid needs oxygen, which is why a starter kept under a layer of water turns lactic [@breadcode-0083; @breadcode-0193].

## pH checkpoints

pH is a logarithmic scale: one unit is a tenfold change. A dough that goes from pH 6.0 to 4.0 has become about a hundred times more acidic [@breadcode-0512; @breadcode-0513].

| Step | pH |
|---|---|
| Starter ready | 4.20 |
| After mixing | 6.00 |
| Dividing and preshaping | 4.10 |
| Shaping | 4.05 |
| Before the final proof | 4.03 |
| After the final proof | 3.80 |
| After baking | 3.90 |

These are example values that the Framework's author measured on his own process [@breadcode-0518; @breadcode-0519; @breadcode-0520; @breadcode-0521; @breadcode-0522; @breadcode-0523; @breadcode-0524]. He ends his bulk fermentation at about pH 4.1 and insists that the figure is personal: starter, flour and water all move it, and a stronger flour tolerates a lower endpoint [@breadcode-1103; @breadcode-0514; @breadcode-0515]. In his videos, he gives 4.2–4.5 for the end of bulk, lower for a sourer loaf and higher for a lighter one [@breadcode-1641], and a reading below about 4.0 signals a risk of over-fermentation [@breadcode-1731]. Novita Listyani uses 4.5 or lower for a mature starter, and about 4 as the point her process heads for [@novita-0393; @novita-0643].

To use pH yourself, record it at each step of a bake that turned out well, then aim for the same values next time [@breadcode-0517]. The meter must suit semi-solid food, compensate for temperature and be recalibrated often; rinse the probe for at least a minute before it touches dough, because its storage liquid is not food-safe [@breadcode-0527; @breadcode-0528]. [Bulk fermentation: knowing when to stop](/en/learn/bulk-fermentation-endpoints) sets pH beside the other endpoints.

The curve flattens near pH 3.8–4 for a reason. In broth, a sourdough lactobacillus grew between pH 3.9 and 6.7, best near 5.5, while the sourdough yeast was indifferent from pH 3.5 to 7 but strongly inhibited by undissociated acetic acid [@ganzlemg1998]. Fermentation limits itself [@breadcode-0426].

## Steering sourness

- **Time.** Acidity rises the longer the fermentation is pushed, whatever the starter; for a milder loaf, ferment for a shorter time [@breadcode-0241; @breadcode-0997]. Fermenting long for tang takes a strong flour or a loaf pan [@breadcode-0981].
- **The state of the starter.** The longer since its last feed, the more acid it carries into the dough [@novita-0213]. Feeding it at a higher ratio, 1:5:5 rather than 1:1:1, lowers that acidity [@breadcode-2640].
- **The type of starter.** By the Framework's account, for the same rise a liquid starter has made the most acid, of the mild lactic kind, and a stiff one the least [@breadcode-0242; @breadcode-0907]. A published comparison of sourdoughs kept for 28 days, firm or liquid, ranked them the other way round for acidity [@dicagnor2014]; see [Maintaining a starter](/en/learn/starter-maintenance).
- **Salt in the levain.** About 2 % salt in the levain, as in the German Monheim process, holds the bacteria back and gives a milder bread; a little salt, up to about 0.7 %, slightly stimulates them [@simonson2003; @novita-0057; @novita-0058].

### Temperature: a disputed lever

Common advice says that a cool fermentation gives a vinegary, acetic bread and a warm one a lactic bread. The Framework's author repeats it and says he could not verify it [@breadcode-0088]. Novita, drawing on a handbook and on a study of low-temperature sourdough, says that cold slows acid production, and that a cold-fermented loaf is more acidic only because it ferments for longer [@novita-0081; @xu2020]. The Bread Code's videos say that yeast copes better with cool temperatures and the bacteria with warm ones [@breadcode-1576]. Published growth curves agree in part: the sourdough yeast was fastest near 27 °C and the lactobacilli near 32–33 °C, but below 26 °C the two responded to temperature alike [@ganzlemg1998]. So above about 30 °C the bacteria gain on the yeast, which tilts a warm fermentation towards acid. None of these sources shows that the fridge, by itself, makes a loaf more acetic; see [Retarding dough in the fridge](/en/learn/cold-retard).

### Baking: the book against the video

Dough tastes far more acidic than the loaf it becomes [@breadcode-0335]. The Framework's explanation is that acetic acid is volatile and leaves with the steam, mostly from the drying crust, while lactic acid stays [@breadcode-0652; @breadcode-0989]. Its evidence is indirect: in a steam separation used in wine analysis, the steam carried at least 99.5 % of the acetic acid and at most 0.5 % of the lactic acid [@breadcode-0654; @vine2015]. Hence its advice that a longer bake gives a milder loaf, and toasting a milder slice [@breadcode-0656; @breadcode-0996].

An earlier video by the same author argued the opposite from boiling points: with acetic acid at about 118 °C and lactic acid at about 122 °C, both far above a crumb at 92 °C, little acid would be lost in a normal bake [@breadcode-2306]. The book now points out that the 122 °C figure for lactic acid is measured under vacuum [@breadcode-0653]. A measurement in that same video points the book's way: a loaf par-baked to a core of 92 °C and finished later read pH 3.70, against 3.85 for its fully baked twin, and tasted more sour (one loaf each, tasted by the baker alone) [@breadcode-2307].

## The price of tang

The acid and the time that give tang also weaken the dough. A very bacterial, over-sour starter makes the whole dough bacteria-dominant, and its excess acid breaks down the gluten: the loaf is less airy and rises less in the oven [@breadcode-0427; @breadcode-1460]. [Troubleshooting the crumb](/en/learn/troubleshooting-crumb) shows what that looks like.

## FODMAPs and gluten: what fermentation does not do

Fructans and other FODMAPs trigger symptoms in some people with irritable bowel syndrome. Sourdough fermentation can reduce them in the laboratory, but the clinical benefit is unclear: in some trials, low-FODMAP sourdough rye bread did not improve the symptom score, and the rest of the diet matters [@novita-0153]. More broadly, two reviews found no clinically established health benefit of sourdough bread over yeast bread [@damico2023; @ribet2023].

Sourdough bacteria do break down part of the flour proteins, and the Framework notes that a fast yeast bread keeps more gluten than a long-fermented one [@dicagno2002; @breadcode-0012]. Less is not none. Sourdough bread made from wheat, rye or barley is not gluten-free: coeliac disease is triggered by the gluten of these grains and its only remedy is a strict gluten-free diet, and D'Amico and colleagues' review found no data to support the claim that sourdough bread reduces the risk of developing it [@damico2023].
