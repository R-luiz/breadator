Hydration is the weight of water in a dough as a percentage of the weight of flour [@breadcode-0830]; [baker's percentages](/en/learn/bakers-percentages) explains the convention. The same percentage does not give the same dough with every flour, because absorption depends mainly on protein and on bran [@breadcode-2449].

## Where the water goes

Published water-binding figures for the same flour component disagree widely: damaged starch 1.75–4 g per gram, gluten 1.35–2.15, arabinoxylans (pentosans) 3.5–15. Adding them up cannot predict a flour [@science-flour-0053]. The measured relations point to four drivers.

- **Protein.** In twenty Canadian flour streams, each 1 % of protein added about 1 point of farinograph absorption [@tipples1978].
- **Damaged starch.** In the same flours, each 4 Farrand units of starch damage added another point, and damage alone explained most of the spread [@tipples1978]; see [starch and gelatinization](/en/learn/starch-and-gelatinization).
- **Bran and pentosans.** Wholemeal flours absorbed 5.5–8 points more than refined flour milled the same way, in hard red spring wheat [@pradhan2026]. Within white flours the evidence is mixed: Tipples found that pentosan differences had no major influence [@tipples1978], whereas in UK flours adding fibre measurements improved the prediction, β-glucan most [@lovegrove2020].
- **The flour's own moisture.** If consistency depends on water per gram of dry matter, a flour absorbing 60 % at 14 % moisture needs about 63.7 % at 12 % and 58.1 % at 15 %: a derived figure, close to the −1.97 points per point of moisture of the Farrand equation [@science-flour-0056; @lovegrove2020].

## What the farinograph figure is, and is not

A mill's water absorption is the water that brings a dough to a standard consistency on a farinograph, 500 units, normally reported at 14 % moisture [@pradhan2026]. The test dough has no salt; in one flour, 2 % salt cut absorption from 62.8 to 58.9 % [@rodriguezdemarco2022]. The figure ranks flours; it is not the hydration of a bread. The same French T65 is used at 62 % in a short straight dough and at 68 % in a long-fermented tradition dough [@bourre2008], and in hard red spring flours baking absorption ran 2–5 points above the farinograph value [@pradhan2026].

Bakers who test a flour by hand measure something else: the most water at which a small test dough still stretches into a windowpane after a rest, from which The Bread Code advises beginners to stay about 5 points down [@breadcode-2374; @breadcode-2453]. One of its recipes uses 65 % water for a 10 % protein flour and 70 % at 12 % [@breadcode-1388], about 2.5 points per point of protein against about 1 on the farinograph [@tipples1978].

## Published relations and their limits

- **Tipples 1978:** absorption = 42.7 + 1.03 × protein + 0.248 × damaged starch, r² = 0.993, from 20 Canadian hard red spring flour streams of 11.5–19.2 % protein [@tipples1978]. It covers one class of wheat, and a damage figure home bakers rarely have.
- **Farrand:** absorption = 68.26 + 0.878 × protein + 0.334 × damaged starch − 1.97 × moisture; it explained 83–86 % of the spread in UK flours [@lovegrove2020]. With damage in % of flour it returns about 53 % for an ordinary flour, so only its slopes are usable [@science-flour-0047].
- **A default for common wheat** without a mill value: about 59 + 8 × (ash % of dry matter − 0.55) + 1.0 × (protein − 11.5), ± 3 points, anchored on a few flours and recipes rather than fitted: a rough estimate [@science-flour-0070].

## A method in four steps

1. **Start from what works.** A recipe that already gives the dough you like carries your process and your consistency. Without one, start from the usual hydration of the [flour type](/en/learn/flour-types): French reference recipes use 62 % for T55, 66 % for T80 and 70 % for T150 [@bourre2008]; an industry handbook gives 60–62 % for wheat bread and 82–86 % for rye bread [@gmbhnd].
2. **Correct for the flour.** Keep the recipe's hydration and add the difference in absorption between the new flour and the old: mill values if you have them, otherwise type estimates shifted by about 1 point per point of protein [@tipples1978] and by the moisture correction above. For blends, a weighted mean was within −0.8 to +2.4 points of an industry table of wheat–rye blends [@science-flour-0057].
3. **Add the water of the additions.** Vital gluten takes about 1.5 points of water per 1 % added [@science-flour-0060]. Bran, seeds and psyllium differ widely: sunflower seeds soak up 50 g of water per 100 g [@science-flour-0068], psyllium husk takes about 9.5 g of extra dough water per gram [@science-flour-0065]; [fibre, whole grain and seeds](/en/learn/fibre-whole-grain-and-seeds) lists them. For a scald, published shifts disagree, from none to about 1 point per % of flour scalded, the larger ones for fully gelatinized starch [@science-flour-0091]; see [tangzhong, yudane and other scalds](/en/learn/tangzhong-yudane-scalds).
4. **Count every gram once.** The water of the levain, soakers and scald is part of the total; the final mix gets what is left, and if nothing is left the stages are too wet [@science-flour-0095]. The [formula calculator](/en/calculators/formula) does this bookkeeping.

The [flour calculator](/en/calculators/flour) follows steps 1 to 3 for a blend: a starting hydration with its range, taken from the flour types or carried over from a recipe that already works, plus the water of additions and of a scald. It also gives the protein of the blend and the vital gluten for a protein target.

## A worked example

A T65 recipe works at 68 %, and 30 % of its flour is to be replaced with T150. The calculator's estimated absorptions, from the default formula, are 60 % for T65 and 67 % for T150 [@science-flour-0070], so the new blend absorbs 0.7 × 60 + 0.3 × 67 = 62.1 %, 2.1 points more: about 70 % water. The estimates carry ± 3 and ± 4.5 points, but only the 30 % that changed counts, which gives about ± 1.6 points. Other sources land inside that band: the French reference recipes put T150 8 points above T65, or +2.4 at 30 % [@bourre2008]; a creator rule of 5 g more liquid per 120 g of wholemeal gives +1.3 [@science-flour-0039]; measured wholemeal gaps give +1.7 to +2.4 [@pradhan2026].

## What the method cannot know

The estimate carries over a difference between flours, nothing else. It cannot see the process, which moved the same T65 from 62 to 68 % [@bourre2008]. It does not know how wet you like a dough: a sticky high-hydration crumb is a matter of taste, and handling it a matter of skill [@breadcode-0403; @breadcode-2453]. Flours of one type differ too: in three Italian wheats milled to the same grades, the varieties differed more than the grades [@direnzo2023]. Treat the result as a first bake and adjust from what the dough and loaf show.
