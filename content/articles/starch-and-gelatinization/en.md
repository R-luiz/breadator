Bread flour is about two-thirds starch [@jang1996; @eliasson1980]. That starch holds part of the dough water, is what the flour's amylases cut into sugars [@bakerpedianda], gelatinizes in the oven to build the crumb [@breadcode-0811], and makes bread stale as it firms again [@gmbhnd].

## Granules, amylose and amylopectin

Starch sits in flour as granules. Wheat starch is about 25 % amylose and 75 % amylopectin [@yamada2015]. Intact granules take up little water, about 40 % of their weight [@science-flour-0037]. Once gelatinized, they swell, burst and release amylose, and can absorb up to about 20 times their dry mass [@novita-0111].

## Damaged starch

Milling breaks part of the granules. Damaged starch is about 1–4 % of the starch in soft-wheat flour and 6–12 % in hard-wheat flour, and it absorbs two to three times more water than intact starch [@science-flour-0037]. In twenty Canadian flour streams, each 4 Farrand units of damage added about 1 point of farinograph absorption [@tipples1978]. Above about 8 % damage, the starch competes with the gluten for water and can disrupt the network [@zhao2026].

Bakers care for a second reason: α-amylase only attacks damaged or gelatinized starch [@bakerpedianda], so in the raw dough damaged starch is what it works on, and *The Sourdough Framework* counts it as food for fermentation [@breadcode-0273]. The effect can turn around: in a long test process with malt, more damage lowered the water the dough could carry in the bake while raising the farinograph figure [@tipples1978]. [Enzymes in dough](/en/learn/enzymes-in-dough) follows the amylases further.

## Gelatinization: when it starts and ends

Heated in water, granules lose their ordered structure, swell and turn into a paste: they gelatinize. For wheat starch in excess water, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) places the onset at 60.7 °C [@woodbury2022]. Starch heated only to the end of that DSC signal, 65.6–67.5 °C, still kept part of its gelatinization energy; none was left after 71.0–73.6 °C [@wang2018]. In a 1:2 yudane, granules lost their birefringence from 65 °C and were fully gelatinized at 75 °C [@yamada2015]. Other methods start lower: 56.1 °C for white wheat flour on an amylograph [@wojciechowiczbudzisz2023], 51–60 °C in a trade encyclopedia's table, which does not state its method [@science-flour-0085]. In plenty of water, the measurements put the start near 60 °C and the end in the low 70s [@woodbury2022; @wang2018].

## Water, sugar and salt move the numbers

**Water.** Studies draw the line for "enough water" differently. One pair puts it at 60 % water (water over water plus starch), above which the crystallites melt near 65 °C [@jang1996; @eliasson1980]. Two others find that below about 66–67 % water a second, higher melting step appears, spreading over 40–50 °C instead of 10–15 °C [@yost1985; @wang2018]. In a dough without salt, gelatinization ran from 60.2 to 79.8 °C [@rodriguezdemarco2022].

**Sugar.** In sucrose solutions of 15, 30, 45 and 60 %, wheat starch started to gelatinize at 65.8, 72.2, 82.4 and 98.5 °C, against 60.7 °C in water; glucose and fructose shift it less [@woodbury2022]. The percentage is the sugar in the water phase: a dough with 20 % sugar and 60 % water has 25 % sugar in its water. Interpolating between the 15 % and 30 % points puts the onset near 70 °C, an estimate rather than a measurement, so a scald made with that sweetened liquid would need to go well above 65 °C [@science-flour-0082].

**Salt.** In dough, 1 % salt moved onset, peak and end from 60.2, 70.8 and 79.8 °C to 61.6, 72.2 and 82.7 °C, and 2 % to 61.7, 72.9 and 84.9 °C [@rodriguezdemarco2022].

## What it means for a scald

A scald gelatinizes part of the flour before mixing; [tangzhong, yudane and other scalds](/en/learn/tangzhong-yudane-scalds) covers the methods. The figures above bear on two disputes.

**65 °C or 75–80 °C.** The classic 1:5 tangzhong is heated to 60–65 °C while stirring [@novita-0846]. Combining the energy left at 65.6–67.5 °C [@wang2018] with the full gelatinization energy of wheat starch [@yost1985] suggests that starch is only about 76–86 % gelatinized at 66–67 °C and needs 71–74 °C to finish: a low-confidence estimate across two studies with different starches and methods [@science-flour-0081]. Novita Listyani, who puts the share at about 90 % at 65 °C, now aims for 75–80 °C [@novita-0013], and a published water-roux formula cooks its paste to 80 °C [@science-flour-0086]. Her own account of the enzymes adds a caveat to the higher target: β-amylase is inactivated above about 70 °C, so more heat means less maltose [@novita-0118]. No side-by-side test in the sources weighs the two.

**The 1:1 yudane.** Novita holds that a 1:1 mix of flour and boiling water gelatinizes well if it stays above 65 °C while mixing [@novita-0764]. In one measured process it did not: a 1:1 yudane mixed for 4 minutes ended at 55 ± 1 °C [@tsuboi2017], below the 60.7 °C onset [@woodbury2022], so only part of its starch can have gelatinized unless the bowl is pre-warmed and the mixing fast [@science-flour-0093].

## In the oven

Inside a baking loaf, IREKS places the gelatinization of wheat starch between 60 °C and 78–89 °C and that of rye starch between 50 °C and 65–76 °C, overlapping the denaturation of gluten at 60–80 °C [@gmbhnd]. Gelatinization is what holds a risen dough: The Bread Code warns that a high-hydration dough baked too cold spreads [@breadcode-1066]. At the surface, steam gelatinizes more starch and gives a glossier, denser crust [@altamiranofortoul2012]; see [baking and steam](/en/learn/baking-and-steam).

## Retrogradation and staling

As the loaf cools, gelatinized starch reorders: it retrogrades. Amylose does so during cooling and sets the first firmness of the crumb; amylopectin recrystallizes slowly during storage and squeezes out water. The amylose part cannot be undone, the amylopectin part can, briefly, by reheating [@gmbhnd]. Retrogradation runs between about −5 °C and 60 °C, fastest near the cold end, so a fridge speeds firming: over 10 days in a plastic bag, wheat pan bread firmed to 424 % of its first-day value at 8.2 °C, against 390 % at 21.9 °C [@alpers2021].

How fast it goes depends on the water present while the bread ages [@yamauchi2014]. Yudane bread hardened more slowly at every storage temperature tested, and the temperature at which its hardening stops fell from 67.6 °C for the control to 58.8 °C with 20 % yudane [@tsuboi2017]. Storage itself is covered in [storing and freezing bread](/en/learn/storing-and-freezing-bread).
