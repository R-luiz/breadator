# scalds — Novita Listyani / Seraphine Lishe

Paraphrased from the creators' videos (all rights reserved; facts only). Every bullet links to a claim in `data/claims-novita.jsonl` (id, video, timestamp, evidence label). Dates are video publication dates — her methods changed over time.

## Synthesis

Her most developed subject, studied in more than 15 videos from 2021 to 2026. Her method changed over that time; the dates below show when.
- **Definition and naming.** Tangzhong (Chinese reading) and yudane (Japanese reading) are the same characters 湯種 and the same method. She expresses the scald as a **% of total flour** ("tangzhong 20" = 20 % of all flour is scalded). Treat it as a derivative of flour plus water, and count both in the totals (novita-0012, novita-0138).
- **Ratio.** 1:1 flour:water is enough to put starch in excess water (≥60 % water w/w). She uses 1:1.5–1:2 because it mixes and holds heat more easily (2022, reaffirmed 2026). She rejects 1:5: it limits the scald to about 5 % of flour, and on the stove it evaporates unpredictably. The one exception is a dilute rice-flour glaze brushed on shio pan.
- **Temperature.** Starch gelatinizes from about 65 °C and is complete around 75 °C in a 1:2 yudane. In 2023 she warned that overshooting 65 °C by about 5 °C inactivates β-amylase (maltose, sweetness). In 2026 she now aims for **75–80 °C**, so that heated gluten raises water holding and volume. Her preferred method is the microwave (1–2 min) or boiling water poured into a pre-warmed bowl.
- **Rest.** 2021–2023: refrigerate 8–24 h and do not use immediately (retrogradation, maltose). 2026: use it **immediately or after 24 h cold**. Avoid about 12 h at room temperature and 48 h in the fridge (Ke Yuwen thesis). Cold tangzhong also lowers the dough temperature.
- **Level.** 10 % = casual optimum; 20 % = milk-bread standard; 30 % = upper threshold; 40 % = danger zone; 50 % = never. Volume falls and proof lengthens as the level rises. Sources disagree on which level is softest: the thesis says 10–20 %, Yamauchi 2014 says 40 %.
- **Water.** Optimal absorption rises from 68 % (control) to 71 % (Y20) to 74 % (Y40); for Y15 interpolate to about 70 %. Her rule: add about 2 points per 10 % of flour scalded above 10 %. 2026 figure: about 72 % at 20 %. The "75 % hydration for tangzhong" rule is a myth.
- **Handling.** Break the tangzhong up in part of the water first. Tangzhong doughs feel stronger but tolerate overmixing less; add the fat after some gluten has developed.
- **Rice.** Rice starch gelatinizes higher, up to about 80 °C, so it goes into the boiling water first. Gelatinized rice starch adds volume.

## Claims (112)


### scalds

- European scalding was mainly used for rye (baking quality, shelf life, sensory), whereas yudane is used on wheat flour for a sweet, soft, springy, chewy crumb. *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0110; [DjQ7EtvzK_w 4:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DjQ7EtvzK_w&t=249s); 2023-09]*
- Chinese tàng miàn (hot-water dough for dumplings/mantou) is not tangzhong: water is never equal to flour and boiling water is added gradually, giving only partial gelatinization and protein denaturation. *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0112; [DjQ7EtvzK_w 6:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DjQ7EtvzK_w&t=417s); 2023-09]*

### scalds.rice

- Unlike gelatinized wheat starch, gelatinized rice starch (rice-flour tangzhong or cooked rice in dough) increases bread volume while improving softness and slowing staling. *[creator-rule, low; novita-0023; [ocBV6-O5SL4 27:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ocBV6-O5SL4&t=1628s); 2026-10]*

### scalds.tangzhong

- Stovetop tangzhong: 1 part flour to 5 parts water, heated on low with constant stirring to 60-65 °C, then removed from heat and cooled in a bowl covered with film touching the surface. It can be used at once or refrigerated; she observed no change after more than a week. *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0781; [a2Bhe16kvCI 5:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a2Bhe16kvCI&t=334s); 2021-10]*
- Stovetop tangzhong and poured yudane gave very similar results in her trials, with barely any differences. *[anecdote, low; novita-0785; [a2Bhe16kvCI 4:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a2Bhe16kvCI&t=259s); 2021-10]*
- Tangzhong and yudane use the same Chinese and Japanese characters (湯種). Yudane is the older Japanese technique, and the term tangzhong spread in English through Yvonne Chen's book '65 Degrees Celsius Tangzhong Bread'. *[literature, medium; novita-0786; [a2Bhe16kvCI 0:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a2Bhe16kvCI); 2021-10 — cites ref:chen-65c-bread-doctor]*
- Tangzhong: 30 g flour whisked into 150 g water, heated while stirring to 60 to 65 °C until thick and smooth, then cooled covered. *[creator-rule, high; novita-0322; [ACHbkOi8esY 0:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ACHbkOi8esY&t=37s); 2021-11]*
- Tangzhong at 1:5 flour to water, heated to 60-65 C while stirring, gelatinises most of the water; it is ready when the mixture thickens fully. *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0846; [t--K8le97fc 2:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t--K8le97fc&t=154s); 2022-05]*
- Tangzhong keeps bread softer for longer and makes the dough easier to handle at higher hydration, because the gelatinised starch absorbs more water. *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0864; [rvWSBsQLZXM 0:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rvWSBsQLZXM&t=49s); 2022-07]*
- Boiling bagels gelatinises the outer surface of the dough, forming a shiny, chewy crust that limits oven expansion and gives a denser crumb. *[creator-rule, high; novita-0330; [j187SM6HwVo 0:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j187SM6HwVo&t=49s); 2022-08]*
- Heat the tangzhong to about 60–65 °C before adding it to the dough. *[creator-rule, high; novita-0609; [B37m_f3qdWE 1:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B37m_f3qdWE&t=81s); 2022-08]*
- Research typically scalds 10–50% of total flour; yudane is best below 30% of total flour with 10% as the practical lower limit; higher levels slightly reduce appearance and volume but substantially enhance flavour, softness and shelf life. *[literature, medium; novita-0123; [_mFYeiLzLpo 4:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_mFYeiLzLpo&t=249s); 2022-10 — cites doi:10.3136/nskkk.62.547, ref:ke-yuwen-2004-thesis]*
- Her early tangzhong breads used only 5–8% of flour scalded (1:5 style), which was too little; at 20% she found superior taste and softness with some loss of height/volume. At 1:5, 20% scalded flour would already contribute 100% hydration, so 1:5 cannot reach useful levels. *[anecdote, medium; novita-0124; [_mFYeiLzLpo 4:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_mFYeiLzLpo&t=282s); 2022-10]*
- Her method (2022): boil the water, pour into a bowl, add the flour gradually while stirring at 1:1.5–1:2 flour:water by weight; keep folding until all flour is hydrated and gelatinized; optionally weigh the finished tangzhong and subtract the flour to know its actual water for exact dough hydration. *[creator-rule, high; novita-0127; [_mFYeiLzLpo 14:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_mFYeiLzLpo&t=894s); 2022-10]*
- To convert any recipe: express it in baker's percentages, choose the % of total flour to scald (e.g. 10%), and move that flour plus 1–2× its weight of the recipe water into the tangzhong; total flour and water stay the same. *[creator-rule, high; novita-0128; [_mFYeiLzLpo 16:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_mFYeiLzLpo&t=965s); 2022-10]*
- Gelatinization is irreversible but water absorption is partly reversible: retrogradation in the fridge (syneresis) and kneading release some water from the scald. Adding tangzhong raises dough resistance but lowers tolerance to kneading. *[literature, medium; novita-0130; [_mFYeiLzLpo 17:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_mFYeiLzLpo&t=1077s); 2022-10 — cites doi:10.3136/nskkk.62.547]*
- Her pizza tangzhong (60 g flour and 120 g boiling water, 40% scald) rests about 24 hours in the fridge before use. *[creator-rule, high; novita-0585; [1R62RANFg4Q 8:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1R62RANFg4Q&t=514s); 2022-10]*
- Adding tangzhong or yudane helps dough retain moisture during a longer, lower-moisture bake, so an electric oven can produce a crisp crust without drying the crumb. *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0578; [KLHi11p2VrE 3:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KLHi11p2VrE&t=205s); 2022-11]*

- (2023) Using gelatinized starch immediately without cold retrogradation gives soft, moist bread but lacks the natural sweetness/"wheat umami"; she did not recommend immediate use. *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0132; [OpK1CeW9xm8 7:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OpK1CeW9xm8&t=458s); 2023-01]*

- Scalding rye flour lowers its amylase activity, which otherwise breaks down starch and makes dough soft and sticky; rice flour has a higher gelatinization temperature than rye, so it is added first in the tangzhong. *[literature, low; novita-0244; [tyoILEdRiK8 7:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tyoILEdRiK8&t=467s); 2024-08 — cites doi:10.1016/s0733-5210(09)80024-6]*
- Rye tangzhong: rye starch gelatinises at a lower temperature than wheat, so a rye scald can be made the day before and refrigerated overnight. *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0319; [qhvJ22U5SBI 5:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qhvJ22U5SBI&t=312s); 2024-09]*
- Tangzhong made from 1:2 flour to boiling water (45 g bread flour to 90 g water, about 15% of total flour) is rested overnight; the rest lets flour amylases break the gelatinized starch into simpler sugars, which sweetens the bread. *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0672; [bToyDv4PdL0 6:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bToyDv4PdL0&t=389s); 2024-09]*
- Tangzhong in this enriched dough is credited with a dough that keeps softer for longer, freezes better, tastes slightly sweeter, retains more water, and has a chewier, mochi-like texture. *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0673; [bToyDv4PdL0 4:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bToyDv4PdL0&t=281s); 2024-09]*
- Tangzhong must be mixed quickly while the water is hot enough, above about 65 °C, so the starch fully gelatinizes. Insufficient heat leaves starch holding less water, and the dough becomes sticky. Making tangzhong the night before and refrigerating it gives the best results. *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0754; [Du35FWaSo1U 7:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Du35FWaSo1U&t=461s); 2024-10]*
- A watery or loose tangzhong usually reflects too low a temperature while it is made. Starch needs excess water near 65 °C to gelatinize; a 1:1 flour-to-water ratio is enough, so temperature is the variable to fix. *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0803; [cDBh6i-NoVE 0:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cDBh6i-NoVE&t=0s); 2024-10]*
- Add the hot water first, then the flour, and mix to hydrate it within about a minute. Adding water in portions cools the mix. In cool rooms, preheat the bowl with hot water and discard it before starting. *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0804; [cDBh6i-NoVE 1:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cDBh6i-NoVE&t=70s); 2024-10]*
- Tangzhong is said to improve crumb tenacity (moisture retention) of the bread; she refers to a thesis and a paper, not named in the transcript. *[literature, medium; novita-0423; [1DWHnkSwD48 6:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1DWHnkSwD48&t=393s); 2025-02]*
- Starch in a tangzhong gelatinizes at about 65 °C; the scald is cooked to that point before it is combined with the dough. *[literature, medium; novita-0353; [UK88XjySuZA 11:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UK88XjySuZA&t=713s); 2025-03]*
- She combined gradual water addition with tangzhong in a bread-flour pizza dough with excellent (subjective) results. *[anecdote, low; novita-0100; [PEoTSDswa58 9:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PEoTSDswa58&t=555s); 2025-04]*
- A dough at nominal 100% hydration with tangzhong handled like a dough at about 80% hydration, which made it easier to work. *[anecdote, low; novita-0651; [gPYzcoOgatI 9:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gPYzcoOgatI&t=585s); 2025-07]*
- Tangzhong makes a high-hydration ciabatta easier to handle (it acts like a lower-hydration dough) and keeps the crumb soft longer, which also helps stop home-oven drying. *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0191; [3x4p_bECQbM 2:54–3:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3x4p_bECQbM&t=174s); 2026-01]*
- Ciabatta tangzhong is 2 parts water to 1 part flour (80 g water to 40 g bread flour), boiling water stirred into the flour immediately until it becomes a thick, glossy paste; cool, then refrigerate overnight. *[creator-rule, high; novita-0192; [3x4p_bECQbM 5:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3x4p_bECQbM&t=348s); 2026-01]*
- A tangzhong that has rested overnight is stiff: break it up with the water in the recipe before adding it to the dough, to avoid lumps. *[creator-rule, high; novita-0193; [3x4p_bECQbM 7:11–7:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3x4p_bECQbM&t=431s); 2026-01]*
- For whole wheat bread, combine gelatinized flour (tangzhong) with extra water: a paper found gelatinized starch plus higher water gives better brown-flour bread. *[literature, medium; novita-0031; [GZEvf4buPf4 8:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GZEvf4buPf4&t=494s); 2026-08 — cites doi:10.1016/j.lwt.2019.02.066]*
- Tangzhong by pouring boiling water into a pre-warmed bowl on a scale and immediately stirring in the flour; cover and refrigerate overnight alongside the poolish. *[creator-rule, high; novita-0037; [DtsBfGiq1H0 1:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DtsBfGiq1H0&t=97s); 2026-08]*
- Tangzhong/yudane generally does not make bread fluffier: the gelatinized starch weakens the gluten network and impairs gas retention, so it tends to reduce loaf volume; the original Japanese/Chinese goal was a soft yet slightly chewy ("mochi mochi") texture, not fluffiness. *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0001; [ocBV6-O5SL4 1:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ocBV6-O5SL4&t=73s); 2026-10 — cites doi:10.1007/s13197-016-2399-1]*
- The popular 1:5 flour:water tangzhong limits the scalded flour to about 5% of total flour, so its effect on volume is negligible; a paper found the loaf with the smallest gelatinized-flour addition kept about 96.4% of control height. *[literature, medium; novita-0002; [ocBV6-O5SL4 6:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ocBV6-O5SL4&t=368s); 2026-10 — cites doi:10.1007/s13197-016-2399-1]*
- In a thesis study, bread made with tangzhong refrigerated for 24 h was among the softest right after baking and had the best anti-staling effect after 3 days; tangzhong used immediately (0 h) was also very soft; tangzhong held 12 h at room temperature or 48 h in the fridge gave harder bread. *[literature, medium; novita-0004; [ocBV6-O5SL4 9:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ocBV6-O5SL4&t=554s); 2026-10 — cites ref:ke-yuwen-2004-thesis]*
- In the same thesis 10–20% tangzhong (share of total flour) gave the softest bread and higher percentages gave increasingly harder bread; another paper found the opposite trend. *[literature, low; novita-0005; [ocBV6-O5SL4 9:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ocBV6-O5SL4&t=593s); 2026-10 — cites ref:ke-yuwen-2004-thesis]*
- Tangzhong rested 24 h cold showed almost no significant volume loss up to 30% addition (thesis); 10% tangzhong used immediately gave a non-significant +0.9% volume. *[literature, medium; novita-0006; [ocBV6-O5SL4 10:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ocBV6-O5SL4&t=631s); 2026-10 — cites ref:ke-yuwen-2004-thesis]*
- Texture differences between tangzhong aging methods are small on bake day (sensory panel had no significant preference) but hardness and chewiness differences widen on days 2–3. *[literature, medium; novita-0007; [ocBV6-O5SL4 11:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ocBV6-O5SL4&t=669s); 2026-10 — cites ref:ke-yuwen-2004-thesis]*
- Heated (partially denatured) gluten in the scald holds significantly more water and, when added to dough, increased bread volume and softness, best for gluten heated to 75–80 °C; earlier studies (and her own earlier videos) had blamed denatured gluten for volume loss. *[literature, medium; novita-0010; [ocBV6-O5SL4 15:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ocBV6-O5SL4&t=923s); 2026-10 — cites doi:10.3136/nskkk.64.90]*
- Make tangzhong with high-protein bread flour: more protein means more heated gluten, expected to give a softer, longer-lasting loaf. *[extrapolation, low; novita-0011; [ocBV6-O5SL4 16:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ocBV6-O5SL4&t=994s); 2026-10]*
- Express tangzhong as % of total flour (20 g of 100 g total flour scalded = "tangzhong 20"). Water: 1:1 is technically sufficient but she uses 1.5–2× the flour weight so it mixes thoroughly and holds heat (e.g. 50 g flour → 75–100 g water). *[creator-rule, high; novita-0012; [ocBV6-O5SL4 17:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ocBV6-O5SL4&t=1028s); 2026-10]*
- Two cooking options: mix flour and water, cover and microwave 1–2 min; or pour boiling water into a pre-heated glass bowl and add the flour, mixing at once. She now prefers the microwave because it reaches higher temperature; target about 75–80 °C rather than the usual 65 °C (where ~90% of starch gelatinizes). *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0013; [ocBV6-O5SL4 17:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ocBV6-O5SL4&t=1068s); 2026-10 — cites doi:10.3136/nskkk.64.90]*
- Her updated 24-hour method: use tangzhong immediately for pretty soft bread, or refrigerate it 24 h for the softest bread and a colder dough; avoid long room-temperature holds and longer fridge holds (e.g. 48 h). *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0014; [ocBV6-O5SL4 19:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ocBV6-O5SL4&t=1142s); 2026-10 — cites ref:ke-yuwen-2004-thesis]*
- Tangzhong level guide (% of total flour): 10% = common casual optimum, improves flavour, softness, anti-staling with little height loss; 20% = sweet spot for shokupan/milk breads and the standard in Japanese papers, very soft, moist, slightly chewy, sweeter, but noticeably lower specific volume and slightly longer proof; 30% = upper threshold, very soft, strong anti-staling, stark volume loss (fine for cinnamon rolls); 40% = danger zone; 50% = severe volume loss, sticky, essentially never used. *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0015; [ocBV6-O5SL4 19:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ocBV6-O5SL4&t=1179s); 2026-10]*

### scalds.yudane

- Yudane is made with boiling water and 20% of the total flour, mixed in before the dough, to gelatinize starch and sweeten the crumb. *[creator-rule, high; novita-0291; [zBTHt0uWUeM description](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zBTHt0uWUeM); 2021-03]*
- Yudane (the Japanese original): pour boiling water over flour at 1:1 and mix immediately. Warm the bowl with boiling water first, pour in the water before the flour, and use at once or refrigerate. *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0782; [a2Bhe16kvCI 9:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a2Bhe16kvCI&t=541s); 2021-10]*
- Yudane aged 3 days in the fridge developed a small but noticeable gluten-related difference compared with fresh yudane, with little visible effect on the bread. *[measured, low; novita-0784; [a2Bhe16kvCI 10:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a2Bhe16kvCI&t=620s); 2021-10]*
- In Japan yudane has no fixed flour:water ratio; equal parts flour and hot water is common, a 1999 patent uses 100 g flour to 80–120 g water, and the 2015 Yamada paper used 1:2 for practicality. The 1:5 "tangzhong" is just a dilute yudane variant popularized by Yvonne Chen's book, with no stated rationale. *[literature, high; novita-0122; [_mFYeiLzLpo 1:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_mFYeiLzLpo&t=70s); 2022-10 — cites doi:10.3136/nskkk.62.547, ref:patent-jp3167692, ref:ke-yuwen-2004-thesis]*
- Yamauchi 2014 protocol: bread flour 12.2% protein, 0.39% ash, 13.5% moisture; yudane made with boiling water and flour 1:1 in a mixer, cooled, then stored at 5 °C for one day before use, at 0% (control) and 20–40% of total flour. *[literature, high; novita-0131; [OpK1CeW9xm8 7:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OpK1CeW9xm8&t=423s); 2023-01 — cites doi:10.3136/fstr.20.1071]*
- A Japanese study found starch paste raised crumb moisture, reduced hardness and increased cohesiveness, and the bread was judged easier to swallow (relevant for elderly or dry-mouth eaters). *[literature, low; novita-0135; [OpK1CeW9xm8 14:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OpK1CeW9xm8&t=869s); 2023-01]*
- Scalding part of the flour (yudane) releases maltose and dextrins that give bread a natural sweetness; a yudane dough is said to be sweeter than a straight dough. *[literature, medium; novita-0475; [qdOHmdTTs24 0:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qdOHmdTTs24&t=29s); 2023-04 — cites doi:10.3136/fstr.20.1071]*
- A cited study reports about 23 mg of maltose per gram of dough in a yudane 40 bread, indicating that scalding raises free sugars. *[literature, medium; novita-0476; [qdOHmdTTs24 1:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qdOHmdTTs24&t=67s); 2023-04 — cites doi:10.3136/fstr.20.1071]*
- For a yudane 20 dough, she considers about 71% hydration the upper limit for a soft, springy crumb. *[creator-rule, medium; novita-0477; [qdOHmdTTs24 3:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qdOHmdTTs24&t=211s); 2023-04]*
- "Tangzhong" (Chinese reading) and "yudane" (Japanese reading) are the same written characters (湯種) and the same method; the term originated in Japan (Ke Yuwen 2004 thesis credits Okumoto Milling as first to use yudane), spreading to Taiwan via 7-Eleven in the early 2000s. English: water roux. *[literature, medium; novita-0109; [DjQ7EtvzK_w 2:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DjQ7EtvzK_w&t=143s); 2023-09 — cites ref:ke-yuwen-2004-thesis]*
- Yamauchi 2014: as yudane level rises, bread-making water absorption and final proof time increase, while gas retention, gassing power and specific loaf volume decrease and crumb grain slightly degrades; bread moisture rises; total saccharides and maltose at yudane 40% are about 2× control; yudane breads (esp. 40%) were softer and staled more slowly. *[literature, high; novita-0114; [DjQ7EtvzK_w 10:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DjQ7EtvzK_w&t=628s); 2023-09 — cites doi:10.3136/nskkk.62.547]*
- A yudane-type scald at high proportion gives a denser, softer loaf with lower specific volume; retarded staling is attributed to it. *[literature, medium; novita-0496; [QuhM5qSQ7T0 30:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QuhM5qSQ7T0&t=1847s); 2023-11 — cites doi:10.3136/fstr.20.1071]*
- Yudane and tangzhong differ in how the water-to-flour ratio is set; she frames the scald as a way to pre-gelatinize part of the flour while keeping the dough workable. *[creator-rule, low; novita-0354; [UK88XjySuZA 11:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UK88XjySuZA&t=713s); 2025-03 — cites doi:10.3136/fstr.20.1071]*
- The patented industrial yudane process (Japanese supermarket white bread "Chojuku") ages the scalded flour mixture cold for 15–72 h (renewed patent 24–72 h); the stated purpose is to stop the loaf caving in, a known downside of the method. *[literature, medium; novita-0003; [ocBV6-O5SL4 8:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ocBV6-O5SL4&t=518s); 2026-10 — cites ref:chojuku-yudane-patent, ref:chojuku-book]*
