## Claims

### gluten

- Gluten is the storage protein of flour; once water is added, its protease enzyme starts converting gluten into amino acids, so gluten is progressively broken down during a long fermentation. [breadcode-0011; SF Ch 1 The history of sourdough; creator-rule]
- Gluten binds into a protein network once hydrated and traps CO2 produced during fermentation; the trapped gas cells expand during baking. [breadcode-0013; SF Ch 1 The history of sourdough; creator-rule]
- During fermentation a wheat dough becomes looser and more extensible, while the kneaded dough is elastic and holds together well. [breadcode-0036; SF Ch 2 How sourdough works; literature]
- Sourdough bacteria consume and break down dough gluten through proteolysis. [breadcode-0077; SF Ch 2 How sourdough works; creator-rule]
- Proteolysis makes sourdough dough stickier but also more extensible; as the gluten is reduced the dough is easier for microorganisms to inflate and rise. [breadcode-0078; SF Ch 2 How sourdough works; creator-rule]
- Sourdough contains less gluten than a pure yeast-based dough because of the bacterial component. [breadcode-0080; SF Ch 2 How sourdough works; literature]
- Wheat flour gives a larger visible rise than rye, because wheat contains more gluten; this difference reflects the flour's gluten, not necessarily more microbial activity. [breadcode-0127; SF Ch 3 Making a sourdough starter; creator-rule]
- Wheat builds gluten better in warm conditions. [breadcode-0220; SF Ch 4 Sourdough starter types, §4.4 Stiff starter; literature]
- Kneading builds gluten bonds, and the more you knead, the stronger they become. [breadcode-0256; SF Ch 5 Flour types; creator-rule]
- During fermentation microbes metabolise gluten; if too much is converted the dough loses its wheat-like structure. [breadcode-0257; SF Ch 5 Flour types; creator-rule]
- The more gluten a flour has, the longer the dough keeps its integrity during fermentation. [breadcode-0260; SF Ch 5 Flour types; creator-rule]
- Replacing about 50 % of the flour with wheat helps the gluten network develop during fermentation and trap more gas. — `{"wheat_pct": 50}` [breadcode-0312; SF Ch 6 Bread types; creator-rule]
- Free-standing wheat bread needs mixing enough to develop a gluten network before shaping. [breadcode-0315; SF Ch 6 Bread types; creator-rule]
- In rye, pentosans prevent gluten bonds from forming properly; with no gluten network, the crumb stays dense and not open. [breadcode-0324; SF Ch 8 Non wheat sourdough; literature]
- Kneading builds a strong gluten network, which is needed to trap the CO2 produced during fermentation. [breadcode-0349; SF Ch 7 Wheat sourdough §7.1 The process; creator-rule]
- Water is absorbed into the gluten matrix as it forms; higher-protein flour can take more water. [breadcode-0391; SF Ch 7 Wheat sourdough §7.4 Hydration; creator-rule]
- Wetter dough stretches more easily and holds its shape for less time; stiff, low-hydration dough holds its shape longer. [breadcode-0393; SF Ch 7 Wheat sourdough §7.4 Hydration; creator-rule]
- Waiting after mixing lets the flour hydrate, so gluten bonds form largely automatically; the waiting period matters more than the kneading time itself. [breadcode-0466; SF Ch 7 Wheat sourdough §7.8 Dough strength > Kneading and waiting; creator-rule]
- A kneading schedule of about 5 min kneading followed by about 15 min waiting can give the same development as a much longer initial knead. — `{"knead_min": 5, "wait_min": 15}` [breadcode-0467; SF Ch 7 Wheat sourdough §7.8 Dough strength > Kneading and waiting; creator-rule]
- Recommended initial kneading flow: homogenize, wait 15 min, knead 5 min, wait 15 min, then test the windowpane; if it fails, knead again; if it passes, optionally bassinage, take an aliquot sample if used, then form a round dough ball and begin bulk fermentation. — `{"wait_min": 15, "knead_min": 5}` [breadcode-0468; SF Ch 7 Wheat sourdough §7.8 Dough strength; creator-rule]
- Glutenin and gliadin bond almost immediately after hydration; disulfide bonds link glutenin's long chains into strong, extensible molecules, while the compact gliadin lets dough flow. [breadcode-0469; SF Ch 7 Wheat sourdough §7.8 Dough strength > Gluten network; literature]
- The longer the gluten network is allowed to form, the more it becomes a web-like structure that traps the gases produced during fermentation. [breadcode-0470; SF Ch 7 Wheat sourdough §7.8 Dough strength > Gluten network; literature]
- Low-hydration no-knead doughs develop strength by resting: after a brief homogenizing mix and a few minutes of waiting, gluten forms without additional kneading. — `{"wait_min": [3, 5]}` [breadcode-0473; SF Ch 7 Wheat sourdough §7.8 Dough strength > No-knead; creator-rule]
- Machine kneading: start on low speed to homogenize, let the flour rest, then continue on higher speed; a well-developed dough releases the sides of the mixing bowl. [breadcode-0476; SF Ch 7 Wheat sourdough §7.8 Dough strength > Machine kneading; creator-rule]
- The stickier a dough is, the less gluten development it has; a sticky dough usually signals an under-developed gluten network. [breadcode-0477; SF Ch 7 Wheat sourdough §7.8 Dough strength > Stickiness; creator-rule]

### gluten.breakdown

- Protease enzymes and bacteria both break down gluten, so dough becomes more extensible as fermentation proceeds. [breadcode-0408; SF Ch 7 Wheat sourdough §7.4 Hydration; creator-rule]
- Fermentation that goes on too long leaves little or no gluten; the dough then becomes very sticky. [breadcode-0409; SF Ch 7 Wheat sourdough §7.4 Hydration; creator-rule]
- Prolonged enzymatic action tears the gluten network; the dough flattens and leaks the fermentation gas, the worst outcome for wheat sourdough. [breadcode-0444; SF Ch 7 Wheat sourdough §7.6 Autolysis; creator-rule]
