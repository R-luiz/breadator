# 11. Nutrition: glycaemic response, resistant starch, β-glucan claims, FODMAP, coeliac disease, minerals

Statements here describe studies (population, dose, effect size, certainty). They are not advice and promise nothing for an individual. Machine-readable values: `model-bread-science.json`, section `nutrition`; claims `science-bread-0111` to `0139`.

## Sourdough and blood glucose

| source | design | result |
|---|---|---|
| [Rolim 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/10408398.2022.2108756) (Crit Rev Food Sci Nutr 64:801, 2024) | meta-analysis, 18 trials in adults to June 2021; comparators pooled: industrial bread **or glucose** | change in serum glucose, sourdough minus comparator: −0.29 (95 % CI −0.46 to −0.12; I² 0 %) at 60 min, −0.21 (−0.32 to −0.09; I² 0 %) at 120 min; certainty low to very low; no change in fasting insulin or PYY; clearest for whole-wheat sourdough. Units not given in the abstract (presumably mmol/L). |
| [Starowicz 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2026.1910648) | systematic review, 27 studies, healthy adults, EFSA dossier framework | evidence for a benefit of sourdough over yeast bread "neither sufficient nor convincing"; no meta-analysis (heterogeneity, risk of bias) |
| [Ribet 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.advnut.2022.10.003) | systematic review, 20 studies, 369 people | healthy people: same recipe with sourdough vs yeast, 3 of 8 comparisons significant, 5 null; whole-grain sourdough vs white yeast bread, 2 of 7 significant |
| [Stamataki 2017](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0007114517000770) | narrative review of human studies | sourdough can modulate the glucose response (organic acids suspected); insulin effect unclear; proofing, cooking, partial baking and freezing also matter |
| [EFSA 2014](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2014.3837) | health-claim opinion, high-fibre sourdough rye bread | 4 studies showed lower responses than glucose, but nearly any carbohydrate food does that; effect not specific, no conditions of use. Reiterated in [EFSA 2025](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2025.9630). |

No meta-analysed difference in glycaemic index (GI units) between sourdough and yeast bread was found; the only pooled effect sizes are Rolim's glucose changes.

## Cooling, freezing, toasting and resistant starch

- In 10 healthy adults, the 2-h glucose area (iAUC) after homemade white bread was 259 mmol·min/L fresh, 179 frozen and defrosted, 193 toasted and 157 frozen-defrosted-toasted (−31 %, −25 %, −39 %); a commercial white bread went from 253 to 183 toasted and 187 frozen-defrosted-toasted ([Burton 2008](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ejcn.1602746)).
- Cooled gelatinised starch retrogrades into type-3 resistant starch; the review gives at least about 12 h of cooling and says reheating below 130 °C does not undo it. Its example is potato, not bread: 0.64 g/100 g cooked, 1.30 after 24 h at 4 °C, 1.65 reheated ([Murillo 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2022.1025993), section 'Storage').
- Steamed bread stored 2 days at 4 °C digested slowest in vitro (note 10; [Li 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/foods13040517)).
- No measured resistant-starch content (g/100 g) of cooled, frozen or toasted bread was found. The app describes the direction of the effect and the study size only.
- EU claim: replacing digestible starch so that resistant starch is at least 14 % of total starch ([Regulation (EU) 432/2012](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32012R0432), Annex, 'Resistant starch'). The claim covers replacement of starch in the recipe; no source read showed storage alone reaching that level in bread.

## β-glucan claims, as worded in Regulation (EU) 432/2012

Two different claims with different conditions (Annex, quoted):

- **Cholesterol** — "Beta-glucans contribute to the maintenance of normal blood cholesterol levels": "The claim may be used only for food which contains at least 1 g of beta-glucans from oats, oat bran, barley, barley bran, or from mixtures of these sources per quantified portion. In order to bear the claim information shall be given to the consumer that the beneficial effect is obtained with a daily intake of 3 g of beta-glucans from oats, oat bran, barley, barley bran, or from mixtures of these beta-glucans."
- **Blood glucose after a meal** — "Consumption of beta-glucans from oats or barley as part of a meal contributes to the reduction of the blood glucose rise after that meal": "The claim may be used only for food which contains at least 4 g of beta-glucans from oats or barley for each 30 g of available carbohydrates in a quantified portion as part of the meal. In order to bear the claim information shall be given to the consumer that the beneficial effect is obtained by consuming the beta-glucans from oats or barley as part of the meal."

EFSA (2025) refused to lower the second condition to 2 g per 30 g: across 21 studies (59 comparisons) effects between 2 and 4 g were not consistent. EFSA also excludes enzymatically degraded β-glucans from its evidence (note 5).

Other bread-relevant claims in the same Annex: arabinoxylan-rich fibre from wheat endosperm (≥ 8 g per 100 g available carbohydrate, the fibre itself ≥ 60 % arabinoxylan) for the glucose rise after a meal; rye fibre for bowel function and barley, oat and wheat bran fibre for faecal bulk, each only for foods "high in that fibre"; wheat bran fibre for faster transit with ≥ 10 g a day.

What this means for a loaf (extrapolation): bread holds about 44–54 g available carbohydrate per 100 g ([Ciqual 2020](https://ciqual.anses.fr/): wholemeal 44.3, average bread 54.4), so the post-meal claim needs about 5.9–7.3 g oat or barley β-glucan per 100 g of bread; the cholesterol claim needs 1 g per stated portion (2 g per 100 g for a 50 g portion). Flour or bran weight is not β-glucan weight.

## FODMAP and fermentation time

- Refined-wheat yeast bread, final proof 60 → 80 min: fructans 338 → 266 mg/100 g dry matter, fructose 221 → 99, excess fructose 130 → 24, total FODMAPs 528 → 358 ([Schmidt 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00217-020-03633-6), Table 8).
- Sourdough in the same study cut fructans but raised mannitol, so total FODMAPs did not fall; refined-wheat breads met the low-FODMAP cut-offs, rye breads (fructans, mannitol) and whole-meal wheat breads (excess fructose) did not, whatever the process.
- Cut-offs per serving used (Varney): 0.15 g excess fructose, 0.3 g oligosaccharides, 0.4 g polyols (0.2 g mannitol or sorbitol), 1 g lactose; a bread serving is about 50 g.
- Rye bread: 90 → 180 min of dough fermentation did not lower fructans; a *Lactobacillus plantarum* sourdough did ([Pejcz 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00217-020-03537-5)).
- Straight doughs leave about 1–1.5 % fructans in wheat bread and 3 % in rye bread; yeast invertase only cuts short fructans (DP up to about 4); conventional sourdough can create mannitol; low-FODMAP bread needs fructanase-active organisms ([Loponen & Gänzle 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/foods7070096)). In an IBS crossover trial a low-FODMAP rye bread (FODMAPs one third of regular, fibre kept at 10 g/100 g) caused less flatulence, pain, cramps and rumbling; a second trial found less breath hydrogen.
- Whole-grain bread with 6 % yeast plus baking powder, 60 min at 40 °C: FODMAPs down by more than 75 % ([Torbica 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/foods14020304)).

App wording (extrapolation): longer or yeast-rich fermentation tends to lower wheat fructans; there is no single time curve across flours; never label a bread low-FODMAP without analysis.

## Coeliac disease

**Sourdough fermentation does not make wheat, rye or barley bread safe for people with coeliac disease** (this includes spelt, einkorn, emmer and khorasan).

- Regular sourdough is not gluten-free; fermentation lowers gluten but not to 20 ppm ([Beyond Celiac](https://www.beyondceliac.org/gluten-free-diet/is-it-gluten-free/sourdough-bread/), archived 8 Jan 2026). Damage can occur without symptoms.
- EU law: "gluten-free" means at most 20 mg/kg gluten as sold; "very low gluten" at most 100 mg/kg for specially processed ingredients ([Regulation (EU) 828/2014](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32014R0828), Annex).
- Experimental detoxification ([Greco 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cgh.2010.09.025)): over 60 days at 200 g a day, ordinary wheat baked goods (80,127 ppm gluten) made 2 of 6 coeliac patients stop, with rising antibodies and intestinal deterioration; extensively hydrolysed flour (2,480 ppm) caused villous atrophy in both patients who ate it; only fully hydrolysed flour (8 ppm), made with selected lactobacilli **plus fungal proteases**, left 5 patients unchanged.
- An acute challenge with a sourdough of 30 % wheat and oat, millet and buckwheat flours, fermented 24 h with selected lactobacilli: 13 of 17 patients reacted to the yeast bread but not to this bread ([Di Cagno 2004](https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.70.2.1088-1096.2004)). Neither process is home or bakery sourdough.

## Phytate and minerals

Sourdough lowers phytate more than yeast (note 5). Whether that improves mineral status is unproven: a 2026 systematic review of 8 human studies called the evidence inconclusive — single meals showed better iron absorption (serum iron +59 vs +30 µg/100 mL for low- vs high-phytate bread; added phytase +50 % and +61 % absorption), but 12 weeks of low-phytate sourdough rye bread did not raise iron status (ferritin 32 ± 7 → 27 ± 6 µg/L; body iron 6.9 → 5.4 mg/kg) ([Nikolaou 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2026.1778997)). In-vitro bioaccessibility is not bioavailability; the colon breaks down most remaining phytate ([Brouns 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14010025), section 5).

## Disagreements

- **Sourdough and glycaemia**: Rolim reports a small pooled reduction (low to very low certainty, glucose among the comparators); Starowicz finds the evidence insufficient; EFSA finds effects against glucose non-specific; Ribet counts mostly null comparisons in healthy people.
- **Fermentation time and fructans**: a longer proof lowered fructans in refined wheat (Schmidt) but not in rye (Pejcz); sourdough lowered rye fructans (Pejcz) but left wheat's total FODMAPs unchanged by adding mannitol (Schmidt).
- **Coeliac disease**: fully hydrolysed experimental sourdough wheat (8 ppm, with fungal proteases) was tolerated (Greco), while ordinary sourdough is not gluten-free.

## Gaps

A GI meta-analysis of sourdough vs yeast bread with GI-unit effect sizes; resistant-starch contents of stored, frozen and toasted bread; the β-glucan lost to endogenous β-glucanase in oat or barley breads.
